# Chapter 8 — The Enemy's Clock

The first symptom was a disagreement of seven nanoseconds.

The national time service reported it as a calibration anomaly. The military network described it as a synchronization fault. The civilian broadcasts ignored it entirely. Seven nanoseconds was too small to interrupt a phone call, alter a train schedule, or make a clock visibly wrong.

But every independent reference system on Earth recorded the same direction of drift.

Adrian watched the readings accumulate across the wall display. Atomic clocks in three continents were disagreeing with their own calibration histories. Some claimed their frequency had changed. Others claimed the reference event used to measure that frequency had happened at a slightly different time. The errors were small, but they were correlated.

Mira stood beside him with a tablet pressed against her chest.

“They're not breaking the clocks,” she said. “They're changing what the clocks agree happened.”

“That's the distinction.”

“Can you prove it?”

“Not yet. But ordinary drift would not create the same structured error across isolated systems. Something is moving the reference.”

At 09:30, the first public record changed.

A weather archive revised the timestamp of a storm that had struck the Bay of Bengal six years earlier. The storm's measurements remained the same, but the record now placed its landfall eleven minutes earlier. The adjustment appeared to be a routine correction from a new data source. Within minutes, a second archive adopted it.

Then a shipping database changed the date of a port closure. A hospital system revised the time of a mass-casualty event. A university repository altered the publication date of a paper that had led to one of the first human countermeasures against the Veyr.

Each change was small enough to look like a clerical correction. Together, they formed a pattern.

The past was being edited from the edges inward.

Adrian ran the changes against the Veyr temporal model. The records did not all move backward by the same amount. They shifted along a set of dependency chains. Events that depended on one another changed in coordinated ways, while unrelated records remained stable.

“The network is testing a transition,” he said.

Mira watched a map of the anomalies spread across the globe. “How long until the transition completes?”

“I can't estimate that from these data.”

“Hours? Days?”

“I don't know.”

She looked at him until he met her eyes.

“I will not invent a number because we need one,” he said.

“Good. Then tell me what we can do.”

They moved the AION concept from the notebook to the laboratory board. The design required a local reference that could be checked without relying on the external time network. A bank of atomic clocks would measure one another, while a superconducting toroidal lattice stabilized their phase relationships. A metamaterial shell would isolate the chamber from electromagnetic and mechanical interference. A speculative boundary-control system would use the Veyr phase signature as a guide—not to imitate their temporal technology, but to establish a local state that did not accept the network's rewritten sequence as authoritative.

The design was incomplete. It had more assumptions than components, and some of its central assumptions could not yet be tested. Still, it gave them a target.

AION had to preserve three things: a reliable internal clock, a record of events inside the chamber, and the biological continuity of the people within it. It could not save the world outside. It could not stop the temporal operation. It could only keep its occupants from being rewritten along with everything else.

At noon, command issued a new order: all research data related to the Veyr temporal anomaly was to be transferred to a central repository. The stated reason was preservation. Adrian read the order twice and noticed that its timestamp preceded the briefing where the repository had first been proposed.

Mira pointed to the line. “That's the same signature.”

“Yes.”

“Then the order may already have been affected.”

“Or the record is wrong. We have to preserve both possibilities.”

They refused the transfer and sent command a signed report explaining why. Within ten minutes, the order was rescinded by a second message whose timestamp was older than the first.

Captain Ilyan arrived in person. “The command network is becoming unreliable. We have orders that appear to have been issued before the meetings that authorized them. Some officers remember receiving them. Others remember the meetings never happened.”

“Do you remember the original sequence?” Mira asked.

“I remember three versions of the same briefing. In one, we approved your research. In another, we shut it down. In the third, the briefing lasted four minutes and no one mentioned your project.”

“Which one is real?” Adrian asked.

Ilyan's expression was grim. “That is what I'm telling you.”

The next shift began without a clear handover. Two technicians arrived claiming they had already completed the day's work. One insisted that a colleague had been evacuated the previous night; the colleague stood beside him, alive and confused. Their records disagreed, then converged on the version that left the fewest contradictions in the facility database.

The human mind was not a reliable instrument for detecting a gradual change to history. People forgot things. Records were amended. Memories filled gaps. The Veyr operation could exploit all three.

Adrian started keeping a handwritten log. Mira did the same. They wrote the date, the time shown by each independent clock, what they had seen, and what they remembered before checking any database. They sealed the pages in separate containers and compared them only after the entries were complete.

“Old-fashioned,” Mira said.

“Harder to update remotely.”

“Unless someone changes the paper.”

“Then we'll have larger problems.”

For a moment, she smiled. Then every clock in the lab stopped.

Not at the same reading. One froze at 12:47:03. Another at 12:47:02. The reference controller showed 12:47:04 and began counting backward. The lights remained on, but the hum of the ventilation system seemed to stretch into a single low note.

Adrian watched the clocks. “It's starting.”

A message appeared on the isolated display. It was not a warning from the laboratory system. It was a fragment of the Veyr phase notation, translated into a string of human-readable terms by the same process that had decoded the navigation unit.

**TEMPORAL CORRECTION WINDOW OPEN**

Mira turned toward the AION sketch.

“How long do we have?”

“I don't know.”

“Then we build as if we have no time at all.”

Adrian erased the board's old schedule. The new plan had no phases for convenience, no optional experiments, no assumption that command communications would remain trustworthy. They would assemble the Stillhouse with whatever components could be secured locally. They would test only the functions that could be tested without exposing the lab to a full temporal pulse.

Outside, the world's clocks continued to disagree by fractions of a second. Inside Lab C, Adrian began to build a machine whose first purpose was not to change time.

It was to remain an honest witness when time stopped telling the same story.
