# Chapter 21 — The Prisoner from Tomorrow

The Veyr did not ask Mira who she was.

They asked what time she belonged to.

Their examination chamber had no visible windows. The walls were layered with dark material that absorbed the light from the scanning instruments. A narrow platform held the remains of her transit device, separated from her by a transparent barrier. The Veyr had removed the outer casing but had not opened the sealed biological container. The sample reservoir was somewhere else on the ship, travelling through a system whose route she could no longer observe.

The first question came through a translation interface that rendered the Veyr's clicks and pulses as a sequence of short phrases.

“Origin reference unknown.”

Mira kept her hands on the table. “My origin is Earth.”

“Earth has no registered temporal contact.”

“Not yet.”

The translator paused, as if deciding whether the answer belonged to a recognised category.

“Your biological structure is human-like. Your temporal signature is incompatible with local history.”

“Then your instruments are working.”

The Veyr examiner tilted its head. It had the same mineral-grey skin as the organism she had studied in the original timeline, but the ridges along its neck moved in a different pattern. Its eyes were dark and reflective. A thin filament extended from its wrist to the examination platform, pulsing in time with the room's lighting.

Mira watched the filament. The same biological coordination signal appeared in the central colony node. The examiner's body and the room's systems were exchanging timing information through contact.

“Are you a weapon?” the translator asked.

“I'm a scientist.”

“Are you a weapon designed to resemble a scientist?”

Mira almost laughed. “That would be an inefficient weapon.”

The examiner did not respond to the joke. It placed a device beside her hand. The device emitted a low pulse, and the room's display filled with a reconstruction of her temporal arrival. It showed a stable field opening, a brief incompatibility, and a return anchor resolving toward the Veyr vessel.

The examiner pointed to the image.

“You arrived before the first human-Veyr contact.”

“Yes.”

“How?”

“I used your technology.”

“Impossible.”

“Your machine disagrees.”

The examiner's filament brightened. For a moment, the phase signal in the room slipped out of rhythm. The examiner withdrew its hand, and the display returned to normal.

Mira recognised the reaction. The Veyr system was not simply reading the temporal signature. It was trying to reconcile a human reference state with a model in which humanity had not yet become relevant. Her presence was an anomaly the system could classify only as a contradiction.

That contradiction was useful. It might keep the Veyr focused on her long enough for LANTERN to spread.

The examination continued for hours. They scanned her cells, her suit, and the remaining parts of the temporal device. They asked about the origin of the technology, the purpose of the sample container, and the meaning of the phrase “not yet.” Mira gave them truthful fragments that did not reveal the deployment sequence. She told them the device had brought her from a future that their records did not contain. She did not tell them what the virus was designed to do.

The Veyr had no reason to trust her, but they had every reason to study her. A human appearing before first contact was a scientific discovery of enormous significance. The examiner's questions shifted from security to biology, then to the possibility that she represented a branch of evolution that the Veyr's models had failed to predict.

They transported her to a secure biological unit. The ship's corridors curved in ways that made distance difficult to estimate. At regular intervals, small membrane panels pulsed as crew members passed. Each interaction produced a brief signal exchange. Mira noticed that the panels were not merely access controls. They were part of the ship's biological timing network.

The sample reservoir had entered the vessel's internal logistics stream.

She could not see it, but she could infer the sequence. A sealed module had been accepted by the service port, scanned as an unknown biological input, and routed toward the ship's containment and transfer system. The Veyr had no reason to assume it was dangerous. The container's outer shell remained intact, and LANTERN's delayed activation was designed to avoid triggering an immediate response.

The ship accelerated.

Mira felt the change through the floor, though there was no conventional engine vibration. The transit system had engaged its FTL field. A sequence of phase pulses travelled through the corridor membranes, synchronising the ship's internal systems for the jump.

The network had begun moving the sample.

She sat on the edge of the examination platform and closed her eyes. She could not know whether the pathogen would survive transit, whether it would establish itself in the first carrier, or whether the Veyr would detect the anomaly before the ship reached its destination. She could only trust the design and the chain of decisions that had brought her here.

The examiner returned before the jump ended.

“Your temporal signature does not resolve,” the translator said. “You may be a contaminant in the historical record.”

Mira opened her eyes. “Then you should handle me carefully.”

The examiner studied her for a long moment.

“Why would a human travel backward before human contact?”

Mira thought of Adrian in the Stillhouse, preserving the logs and watching for a change in the clocks. She thought of the world that had forgotten every person she had ever known. She thought of the quiet Earth beneath her, safe only because the Veyr had not yet decided to erase it.

“To prevent a future,” she said.

The translator rendered the words. The examiner did not understand them, but it recorded them.

The ship emerged from FTL at a remote Veyr system. The crew began unloading biological cargo and equipment for transfer to a larger vessel. Mira watched the process through a narrow observation panel. The sample reservoir was among the sealed containers moved to the next ship. The network would carry it onward, not because it was a weapon, but because the Veyr had classified it as material worth studying.

The first transfer was complete.

The outbreak had not begun in any observable way. No alarm sounded. No Veyr collapsed. The ship's systems reported normal function.

Mira's only evidence was a tiny shift in the timing pattern of one membrane panel as a worker passed.

It could have been noise.

She memorized the pulse and waited for the next.
