# Chapter 2 — The Laboratory at the Edge of the War

The clocks agreed for six hours.

Adrian considered that a kind of progress.

At 19:40, the surface command network lost contact with the western defence corridor. At 20:05, the emergency generator took over the laboratory's power bus. At 21:12, a message arrived from the capital ordering all research teams to prioritise immediate military applications. At 22:00, Mira brought Adrian a cup of coffee that smelled like burnt wiring and told him he had been staring at the same eleven microseconds for so long that the screen would soon file a complaint.

He had not moved the event log to the central server. He had not connected the specimen instruments to the facility network. He had, however, copied the data to three isolated drives and run the same analysis through four independent timing models.

Every model returned the same result.

The measured effect preceded its recorded cause.

“Perhaps the command timestamp is wrong,” Mira said. She stood on the other side of the bench, holding her own cup with both hands. Her field suit had been replaced by dark trousers and a grey laboratory shirt, but the torn sleeve still lay folded beside the specimen case. “That would be the least dramatic explanation.”

“It would also be convenient.”

“Convenience is not a disqualifying property of an explanation.”

“No. But it doesn't make it true.”

Adrian enlarged the event trace. The clocks had not drifted gradually. Their frequencies remained stable, their error margins within tolerance. Then, for eleven microseconds, the reference signals disagreed about the ordering of two events. Afterward, they returned to agreement as if nothing had happened.

The anomaly was small enough to fit inside a single line of data. It was large enough to make the rest of his career look like a warm-up.

Mira put her coffee down. “Show me the comparison with the sample.”

He opened the second dataset. The black fragments from the Veyr transport had shifted their internal phase 0.8 microseconds before the laboratory's magnetic field changed. The clock anomaly and the sample response were not simultaneous, but their signatures shared an irregular pattern: a slight reversal in phase, a brief loss of coherence, then a clean return.

“Could the sample be affecting the clocks?” she asked.

“Not from across the lab. They're on separate power and shielding systems.”

“Could the clocks be affecting the sample?”

“Same answer.”

“So something else is affecting both.”

“Or we're misunderstanding both measurements.”

Mira gave him a look.

“I said it was possible,” he replied.

“You say possible when you mean you hope it's true.”

“That's an unfair assessment of my scientific process.”

“It's an accurate assessment of your face.”

He rubbed his eyes. “What did the field team recover besides the fragments?”

“Three pieces of hull material, one damaged navigation unit, a sealed biological canister, and a Veyr body.”

“Body?”

“Mostly.”

He looked up.

“The recovery team couldn't determine whether the tissue was dead or in some kind of suspended state. It was moved to containment at the xenobiology annex. I asked for a sample. Command refused.”

“On what grounds?”

“Security. They think anything alive from a crashed ship might be a weapon.”

“They may be right.”

“They may also be throwing away our only chance to learn what the enemy is.”

Adrian turned back to the event log. “I'll speak to containment.”

“You'll do more than speak. You'll ask for access, they'll say no, you'll explain why they're wrong, they'll say no more firmly, and you'll return here pretending the conversation was productive.”

“You've made a detailed model of my behaviour.”

“I have excellent observational data.”

He wanted to answer with something clever. Instead, he noticed that she had not touched her coffee.

“Have you slept?” he asked.

“Have you?”

“That wasn't the question.”

“Then it was a badly designed question.”

They looked at each other across the bench. Outside the reinforced walls, the war continued in distant thuds and brief tremors. In the lab, the air system hummed. The little status lights on the isolated instruments made the room feel like a ship drifting through deep water.

Mira's expression changed first.

“I slept in the transport on the way back,” she said. “Forty minutes. Maybe.”

“That's not enough.”

“Neither is your coffee.”

“Mine is a scientific instrument.”

“Yours is a crime.”

This time he did smile, and she seemed relieved to have found it.

The containment officer denied them access at 23:17.

At 23:19, Mira asked whether the officer wanted to be remembered as the person who kept two scientists from examining an alien organism during an extinction-level war.

At 23:21, the officer said that was an unfair use of the phrase extinction-level.

At 23:22, Adrian presented the anomaly report.

At 23:25, the officer let them through under escort.

The xenobiology annex occupied a deeper level of the complex, where the walls were thicker and the air pressure could be isolated by section. Two technicians monitored the sealed biological chamber from behind a transparent barrier. Inside, the Veyr lay on a black examination platform, long-limbed and narrow through the torso. Its skin was not the silver armour plating popular in propaganda images, but a muted, mineral grey, crossed by fine ridges that shifted almost imperceptibly with each mechanical pulse from the life-support unit.

Mira stopped just inside the observation room.

“It's breathing,” she said.

“Barely,” the containment officer replied. “We don't know if that's voluntary.”

“Any response to stimuli?”

“Light, no. Sound, uncertain. Temperature changes produce a small shift in the surface ridges.”

Mira moved closer to the barrier. “Could be a pressure response.”

“Could be a threat display,” said the officer.

“Those aren't mutually exclusive.”

Adrian watched the biological readout. The Veyr's circulation was unlike anything he had seen in the captured tissue reports. Their blood analogue carried oxygen, but also a dense suspension of engineered particles that appeared to regulate cell repair and electrical signalling. The organism's nervous system was distributed in a way that made the distinction between brain and body less useful than he was comfortable admitting.

Mira read the same display. “It isn't simply an animal with unusual anatomy. Its systems are integrated.”

“Like the hull fragments,” Adrian said.

“Maybe. But let's not marry two anomalies because they both look mysterious.”

“I thought you were the one who wanted me to be excited.”

“I want you to be careful in an interesting direction.”

She requested a surface swab and a small blood analogue sample. The officer approved the swab and refused the second. Mira negotiated until the officer allowed a tiny sample from fluid already collected by the life-support system.

Back in Lab C, she placed the sealed vial beneath a molecular scanner. Adrian remained beside her, working through the Veyr navigation unit's recovered signal logs on a separate offline terminal. The unit was damaged, but its final minutes of data had survived. The craft had not been falling blindly. Its navigation system had been trying to correct for a spatial discrepancy that the onboard clocks registered before the discrepancy appeared.

Adrian read the trace twice.

“That's not a crash,” he said.

Mira did not look away from her scanner. “What is it?”

“A failed correction. The ship was trying to compensate for a position it hadn't reached yet.”

“Or the recorder is damaged.”

“Possible.”

“You sound disappointed.”

“I am.”

“That's progress.”

The scanner chimed. Mira's face tightened.

“What?” Adrian asked.

She magnified the molecular profile. The Veyr's repair particles were arranged in repeating clusters, each cluster linked to a set of proteins that appeared to regulate the organism's response to injury. One small family of proteins behaved differently from the others. Its concentration changed whenever the scanner's timing reference slipped by even a fraction.

“Could be a measurement artifact,” Adrian said.

“I know.”

She ran the scan again, this time with a second reference clock. The result repeated.

The protein family was not merely associated with repair. Its shape changed in response to the phase of the reference signal, as if the cells were using an external timing cue to coordinate their internal processes.

Mira sat back slowly.

“What does that mean?” Adrian asked.

“It means we have a hypothesis, not a result.” She tapped the screen. “If this organism's cells depend on a shared timing signal to coordinate repair and communication, disrupting that signal could cause system-wide failure. But we don't know whether the signal is biological, technological, or both.”

“Could the black material be part of the same system?”

“Could be. The fragments may have carried a timing reference for the ship. The organism may have adapted to it. Or we're seeing two separate things that happen to interact with the same anomaly.”

Adrian looked at the paired datasets. For the first time, the impossible ordering seemed less like a ghost and more like a physical problem with edges.

“We need to measure the phase response without the sample,” he said. “Then with the sample. Then with the navigation unit's recovered component, if we can isolate its reference crystal.”

“And I need a larger biological sample.”

“The containment officer will enjoy that.”

“I'll bring coffee as a diplomatic offering.”

“Don't. You'll poison them.”

She leaned against the bench, her exhaustion briefly visible again. The war had taken away ordinary time: meals, sleep, unplanned conversations, the certainty that a person who left a room would come back. Every hour was borrowed from an emergency. Yet she still looked at the data with the same fierce attention she had brought to the crash site.

Adrian realized that he trusted her not because she was fearless, but because she knew what fear was and continued to look.

“You were right about one thing,” he said.

“Only one?”

“The samples and the clocks may be responding to the same phenomenon.”

Mira's smile was small. “I will record this historic concession.”

“Don't get used to it.”

“I wouldn't dream of it.”

A tremor passed through the laboratory. A shelf rattled; one of the suspended fragments turned slowly in its case. On the offline terminal, the recovered navigation log refreshed without receiving any new data.

A new line had appeared.

**REFERENCE EVENT CONFIRMED: LOCAL CAUSAL FRAME UNSTABLE**

Adrian froze.

Mira came to stand beside him. Neither touched the keyboard.

“Did the terminal have a live connection?” she asked.

“No. I disconnected the interface.”

“Then how did it update?”

He checked the system clock. It had not advanced. The line was not in the original file checksum, and no process log showed a write operation.

The text remained on the screen for three seconds, then vanished.

Adrian's pulse was loud in his ears.

“We need to shut the system down,” Mira said.

“Yes.”

“Not restart. Physically isolate it.”

He pulled the power interlock. The terminal went black.

For a moment, they listened to the silence left by its cooling fan.

“What is a local causal frame?” Mira asked.

“A phrase for a condition we don't know how to describe.”

“Did you write it?”

“No.”

“Did anyone on your team?”

“Not that I know of.”

“Then someone—or something—has a vocabulary for this.”

Adrian stared at the dead screen. He thought of the Veyr formation changing course toward the research corridor. Of a ship correcting for a position it had not reached. Of alien tissue reacting to a reference signal no instrument had deliberately supplied.

The simplest interpretation was no longer that time had malfunctioned.

It was that someone had learned to manipulate it.

And whatever the Veyr were doing to the sky, their own technology might not be stable enough to protect them.

Mira picked up her untouched coffee, considered it, and set it down again.

“We should tell command,” she said.

“We should tell them what we know.”

“And what do we know?”

Adrian looked at the isolated drives, the sealed specimen case, the dead terminal.

“That the invasion may be built around a technology we don't understand. That the technology is affecting local measurements of cause and effect. And that the Veyr may have biological systems coupled to it.”

“Not that they can change history.”

“No. Not yet.”

She nodded. “Good. Let's not outrun the evidence.”

For the first time that night, the words sounded like something they both believed.

Adrian began drafting the report. Mira returned to the scanner and started a fresh control series, refusing to let the strange result become a conclusion simply because it frightened them.

At 02:06, the laboratory clocks disagreed again.

This time, every instrument recorded the same missing second.

And on the sealed specimen case, the branching light inside the black fragments flickered once—like a signal answering from very far away.
