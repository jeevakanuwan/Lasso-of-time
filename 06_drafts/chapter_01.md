# Chapter 1 — The Sky Was Already Occupied

The first thing Adrian Sen learned about the invasion was that the sky could be crowded without looking full.

From the observation window six floors below the surface, the morning above Colombo looked almost ordinary. Cloud bands drifted over the Indian Ocean. Sunlight flashed on the water. A passenger aircraft, its transponder dark under wartime restrictions, crossed the horizon on a long, cautious arc.

Then a point of light appeared where no point of light belonged.

It did not fall. It did not burn. It simply became brighter, as if something had turned a lamp toward the planet from the other side of the sky.

“Track seventeen,” said the operations officer behind him. “Same signature as last Thursday.”

Adrian watched the point divide into three.

“Last Thursday's signature was atmospheric interference.”

“Last Thursday's interference destroyed a radar station.”

“Correlation isn't causation.”

The operations officer gave him a tired look. “You scientists are going to get us all killed with that sentence.”

A white line appeared across the clouds. It was too straight to be lightning and too broad to be a contrail. For a moment it held still, an incision in the blue. Then the air around it rippled.

The facility shuddered.

Somewhere above them, a weapon had struck the coastal defence grid. The lights dimmed and returned. Dust sifted from a ceiling vent. A siren began its three-note cycle, stopped, and began again in a lower register.

Adrian's tablet filled with red warnings.

“Please tell me that isn't your experiment,” the operations officer said.

“My experiment is underground.”

“Comforting.”

Adrian was already moving.

The laboratory occupied a buried complex built beneath an old research campus, with reinforced access corridors and enough shielding to keep the instruments running through most of the attacks. It had been designed for particle physics. It now housed whatever branches of science the war had not yet made obsolete.

In the corridor, soldiers guided a group of technicians toward the interior shelters. A young man sat on the floor holding a pressure dressing against his forearm. Nobody looked at him for long. The first months of the invasion had made every injury an emergency. The years that followed had made emergencies routine.

Adrian reached Lab C and found the outer door open.

Mira Vale stood over a stainless-steel specimen case, her dark hair tied back with a strip of blue fabric. She wore a sealed field suit with one sleeve torn at the elbow. A smear of black material marked her glove.

She looked up when he entered.

“You're late.”

“There is an active orbital attack.”

“I noticed. The ceiling tried to fall on me.”

“Then I recommend not standing under it.”

“I recommend you stop giving advice to people who have been collecting alien tissue in the rain.”

She turned the case so he could see the contents. Three fragments floated in a clear suspension medium. They looked like slivers of dark glass, except that light moved through them in branching patterns that seemed to turn away from the microscope lamp.

Adrian forgot the sirens.

“Where did you get these?”

“From the evacuation zone outside Trincomalee. A Veyr transport came down hard enough to cut a furrow through two buildings. The soldiers got the crew. We got the debris.”

“You went into a crash site during an attack?”

“I went into a crash site after the attack. There's a difference.”

“Not a meaningful one.”

“Tell that to the three soldiers who brought me the case.”

He leaned closer to the specimen. The branching light changed direction.

“It's responding to the lamp.”

“No. That's what I thought at first.” Mira tapped the side of the case. “Watch the timing.”

She switched off the microscope lamp. The branching faded, but did not vanish. After a pause, it brightened again.

Adrian watched the instrument readout. “Delayed emission?”

“Maybe. Except the delay isn't constant. It changes when I alter the magnetic field.”

“Thermal?”

“I controlled for that.”

“Mechanical stress?”

“Also controlled.”

He looked at her.

Mira's expression softened by a fraction. She enjoyed this part: the instant before someone stopped trying to be right and started trying to understand.

“It's not just a material,” she said. “It behaves like a system.”

“Everything behaves like a system.”

“Then you should be excited.”

“I am trying to be scientifically cautious.”

“You're trying to be emotionally cautious. Scientifically, you're already excited.”

The alarm changed pitch. The laboratory door slid shut behind them as the facility entered lockdown. A moment later, the overhead speakers announced that the surface elevators were suspended until further notice.

Mira glanced up. “How long?”

“Depends on what hit the defence grid.”

“That's not an answer.”

“It's the only one I have.”

For the first time since he had entered, she looked tired. Not frightened, exactly. Tired in the way everyone had become tired: a fatigue too large to be fixed by sleep.

Adrian knew the expression. He had seen it on soldiers, technicians, ministers on emergency broadcasts, and his own face reflected in dark screens.

He lowered his voice. “Are you hurt?”

“Only in ways the medical system doesn't know how to code.”

“Mira.”

She looked down at the torn sleeve. “The suit caught a piece of the wall. I didn't.”

He nodded, accepting the answer because the alternative was to ask again and hear the same thing.

They moved to the central bench. Mira brought up the analysis from the specimen fragments. The material had no stable crystalline lattice, at least not one the instruments could resolve. Its internal structure shifted in response to electromagnetic fields, but not like a conventional metamaterial. The shifts appeared to anticipate the field changes by a fraction of a second.

Adrian leaned over the data.

“That is impossible.”

“That's why I called you.”

“It could be a clocking artifact.”

“I thought of that.”

“Or an aliasing error in the sensor array.”

“I thought of that too.”

“Or—”

“Adrian.”

He stopped.

“I'm not asking you to believe in miracles,” she said. “I'm asking you to look at the data before you explain it away.”

He looked.

The signal repeated across three instruments. Its phase shifted before each field adjustment, as if the material was responding to a condition that had not yet reached it.

He felt a cold, precise excitement open behind his ribs.

“Can you reproduce it?”

“Not reliably. It seems to need an external reference.”

“An external reference to what?”

“That,” Mira said, “is why I called you.”

The floor trembled again. This time the impact was distant. The lights stayed on, but every monitor blinked at once.

A warning appeared across the main display:

**UNSCHEDULED TEMPORAL OFFSET — REFERENCE CLOCK DISAGREEMENT**

Adrian straightened.

“That isn't one of your instruments?”

“No.”

“Did you install a new clock?”

“No.”

“Did anyone change the timing bus?”

“Not without me signing off.”

The warning vanished. The clocks returned to agreement. A line of diagnostic text remained at the bottom of the screen, then cleared itself.

Mira looked from the display to Adrian. “What was that?”

He was already opening the event log.

“Probably a synchronization fault.”

“You don't sound convinced.”

“I don't like conclusions before evidence.”

“That's your favorite sentence.”

“It's a good sentence.”

“It is. You use it whenever you're scared.”

He turned toward her, ready to object. The words never arrived. She had said it without accusation, and because she was right, he had no useful defence.

For a moment the sirens seemed very far away.

Then the log opened.

The disagreement had lasted eleven microseconds. Every clock in the laboratory had recorded the same event, but the timestamp of the event was earlier than the command that had triggered the measurement.

Not by much.

By enough.

Adrian copied the data to an isolated drive.

Mira watched him. “What does it mean?”

“I don't know.”

“Good.”

He looked up.

“That's the first honest answer you've given me all morning,” she said.

Outside, the sky flashed again. The building groaned around them. Somewhere on the surface, people were running toward shelters while the world tried to learn the shape of its enemy.

Adrian stared at the isolated event log.

The material in Mira's case had reacted before the field changed. The clocks had disagreed before the measurement was triggered. Two unrelated systems had displayed the same impossible ordering: effect before cause.

He saved a second copy.

“Don't send this to the central analysis server,” he said.

“Why not?”

“Because I don't know what it is yet.”

“That is not a reason to hide it.”

“It's a reason to keep the raw data intact.”

Mira studied him for another second, then nodded. “Fine. But if you disappear into a mathematics cave for three days, I'm coming in after you.”

“I don't have a mathematics cave.”

“You have a room with no windows and a sign that says unauthorized personnel will be vaporized.”

“The sign is a joke.”

“It is not a good joke.”

He almost smiled. Almost.

The alert system sounded again, this time with a new message from the surface command network. The Veyr formation had changed direction. Instead of continuing toward the coastal defenses, several craft were moving inland toward the research corridor.

Toward them.

Mira closed the specimen case.

“They know what we're doing,” she said.

“We don't know that.”

“They changed course directly toward the research zone.”

“They may be targeting the power infrastructure.”

“Adrian.”

He looked at the black fragments floating in their clear suspension medium. Their internal light had gone still.

For the first time, he considered the possibility that the Veyr were not simply attacking Earth.

Perhaps they were studying it.

Perhaps the war was an experiment.

And perhaps humanity was not the only species trying to understand what had happened to time.

Adrian slipped the isolated drive into his pocket.

“Lock the samples down,” he said. “No network access. No external power controls. Nothing that can change the field without a physical confirmation.”

Mira sealed the case.

“What are you going to do?”

“Find out why our clocks think the future happened first.”

She gave him a grim, exhausted smile.

“Try not to fall in love with the problem.”

He looked at her a second longer than he meant to.

“Too late,” he said.

The next impact shook dust from the ceiling. Neither noticed when the other alarms began.

Adrian had found something the Veyr might not understand.

Mira was afraid that understanding it might be worse.
