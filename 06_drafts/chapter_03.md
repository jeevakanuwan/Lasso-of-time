# Chapter 3 — Two Ways to Kill an Alien

The first proposal to kill the Veyr was not a weapon.

It was a spreadsheet.

Mira projected the table onto the wall of Lab C at 08:10, beneath the official casualty estimates and above a coffee stain that had survived three cleaning crews. The columns listed observed Veyr biology, suspected dependencies, confidence levels, and the kinds of experiments required to test each hypothesis.

Adrian read the first line.

“Cellular repair coordination.”

“Not just repair,” Mira said. “The same protein family appears in the circulation, the nervous system, and the tissue around the major organs. It may be part of a signalling architecture. The Veyr don't seem to separate their biological systems the way we do.”

“You're proposing a pathogen that attacks a coordination mechanism.”

“I'm proposing that we investigate whether one exists.”

“Those are not the same sentence.”

“Which is why the first column says hypothesis.”

She enlarged the diagram. The Veyr's unusual repair particles were linked to proteins that changed configuration in response to tiny shifts in the sample's timing reference. The effect was subtle. No single measurement proved the organisms relied on an external phase signal. But the same pattern had appeared in three tissue samples and in the material recovered from the transport.

“Two ways to kill an alien,” Mira said. “Break the body, or break the system the body depends on.”

“And the second way risks being wrong in ways we don't understand.”

“So does the first. Bombs don't ask whether the target has a family.”

Adrian turned from the wall. “A pathogen could mutate. It could cross-react with Earth's biology. It could persist after the war. We could win and spend the next century cleaning up the weapon we made.”

“Then we design it to require a Veyr-specific receptor, a Veyr-specific metabolic substrate, and a combination of signals that doesn't exist in terrestrial organisms.”

“That's three locks.”

“Three locks are better than one.”

“Unless the organism changes the locks.”

Mira folded her arms. “Then we make the locks part of a system they can't change without damaging themselves.”

For a moment, neither spoke. She was not asking him to stop worrying. She was asking him to turn worry into engineering.

Adrian studied the diagram again. “If this signalling architecture is real, it may be connected to the navigation technology.”

“That is my suspicion.”

“Which means a biological intervention could affect their equipment.”

“Or their equipment could affect the pathogen. We have to test both directions.”

He looked at the casualty figures on the screen. The invasion had begun less than a year ago, but the official estimates had already become ranges so broad that they seemed designed to hide the fact that anyone was counting. Cities had been evacuated, ports abandoned, and entire regions cut off from reliable communications. The Veyr did not need to occupy every place. They only needed to make resistance expensive enough that people stopped coordinating it.

“Command will ask for a schedule,” he said.

“Command can ask for a pony.”

“They'll ask for a weapon.”

“Then we give them a research plan and refuse to lie about how far along we are.”

The first meeting with the military science council lasted twenty-three minutes and ended with an officer asking whether the pathogen could be ready in thirty days.

Mira said no.

The officer asked whether it could be ready in sixty.

Mira said that was not how biology worked.

The officer asked whether biology could be made to work faster.

Adrian watched Mira's jaw tighten and stepped in before the room could become a contest of authority.

“We can identify candidate vulnerabilities in weeks,” he said. “A safe, species-specific intervention requires testing. If you deploy something uncharacterized, you may create an enemy that adapts faster than we can understand it.”

“We are already facing an enemy that can cross interstellar distances,” the officer replied.

“Which is why we should not give it an unpredictable biological weapon to study.”

That won them a temporary allocation of resources, though not the unrestricted access Mira wanted. The council approved a secure bench, two additional technicians, and a limited sample transfer from containment. It also attached a military observer to every experiment.

The observer, Captain Ilyan, took notes in silence and asked questions that were sometimes more perceptive than Adrian expected.

“What happens if the pathogen doesn't kill them?” he asked during the first planning session.

Mira paused. “It might disable the repair network. That could incapacitate them. It could also do nothing.”

“Could it make them more dangerous?”

“Yes.”

“Would you tell us if you thought that was likely?”

“I'd tell you if the data supported it.”

Captain Ilyan studied her. “I can work with that.”

The first tests used nonliving tissue cultures and synthetic models. They measured binding, response, and breakdown under controlled conditions. Nothing was released, and nothing capable of reproducing was yet constructed. Every candidate pathway failed at least one of the three locks Mira had proposed.

On the fourth day, one model did something unexpected.

It did not destroy the simulated repair network. It caused the network to lose synchronization.

The effect spread through the model as a wave of small delays. Each subsystem continued to function, but none of them agreed on when the others had acted. Repair signals arrived after the damage response. Electrical pulses reached the wrong phase of a cycle. The system did not collapse all at once. It became less and less able to coordinate itself.

Adrian watched the simulation repeat.

“That's not a kill mechanism,” he said.

“Not by itself,” Mira replied.

“It's a timing failure.”

“It's a candidate vulnerability.”

“If their navigation system uses the same architecture—”

“We don't know that it does.”

“But if it does, this could affect more than their bodies.”

Mira saved the result under a neutral label. “Then we need to find out whether the biological and technological systems share a dependency. We don't get to assume they do just because the story would be elegant.”

“I'm beginning to think you enjoy saying that.”

“I enjoy saying it when you're about to skip three experiments.”

Captain Ilyan leaned forward. “If this works, can we stop their ships?”

“We don't know,” Mira said. “And I won't call a model result a weapon.”

The captain accepted that answer, but Adrian noticed he did not stop taking notes.

That evening, a new intelligence packet arrived from a field team that had entered the remains of a Veyr relay station. The data had been recovered from a damaged console and translated by the military's machine-analysis system. Most of it concerned troop movement, supply schedules, and local atmospheric conditions.

One fragment was different.

It contained a list of dates.

Not Veyr dates. Earth dates, mapped to human calendar conventions by the translation system. Some referred to events that had not happened yet: a power-grid failure in southern India, the evacuation of a coastal city, an attempted counterattack that command had not approved. The final entry referred to the destruction of the relay station itself.

The timestamp on the file was four days older than the first event in the list.

Adrian read it twice. “Could be a prediction model.”

Mira stood beside him. “Could be.”

“Could be human intelligence data they stole.”

“Some of those events haven't happened.”

“They may be planning them.”

“Then why list the relay station's destruction before the field team reached it?”

He opened the metadata. The file was not a forecast generated by a local console. It had been stored in a sealed diagnostic partition, protected from ordinary updates. Its internal signature matched the phase irregularities found in the black fragments.

Mira's face had gone still.

“They're not just studying our biology,” she said.

Adrian looked at the dates. “They're studying our history.”

“Or a history they expect to happen.”

Outside the lab, a transport engine rumbled through the underground access tunnel. Somewhere on the surface, people were preparing to fight battles the enemy appeared to have already recorded.

Adrian copied the packet to an isolated drive.

The pathogen project had begun as a question about how to kill an alien. Now the question had changed.

If the Veyr could obtain information from events that had not yet occurred, then any plan humanity made might already be part of the enemy's data.

And if the Veyr were willing to attack the future as well as the present, a weapon that only killed bodies might not be enough.
