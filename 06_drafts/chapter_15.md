# Chapter 15 — The Veyr Colony

The central node stood inside a district that the Veyr maps called a living exchange.

Adrian and Mira reached it during the low-traffic phase, when the transport lanes dimmed and most of the nearby maintenance activity moved indoors. They had spent the previous day studying the pulse pattern from the Stillhouse, identifying the brief interval when the node's external signal dropped below the local noise floor. The interval lasted twenty-two seconds. They would have one chance to cross the service court, collect a biological sample, and return before the next synchronization pulse.

They carried no active transmitter. Their instruments were optical, mechanical, or powered by isolated batteries. Mira wore a sealed field layer with a small sample cartridge at the wrist. Adrian carried the passive recorder and a compact phase detector assembled from parts salvaged from the Stillhouse.

The city was quiet in a way the old Earth had rarely been. Veyr transport moved without engines. The pale structures along the lane pulsed in a slow rhythm, their surfaces brightening as vehicles passed. There were no visible crowds, no advertisements, no human signs of urgency. The infrastructure appeared to anticipate demand and adjust before congestion formed.

“Efficient,” Adrian whispered.

“Don't compliment it,” Mira said. “It erased us.”

“I can acknowledge a system's function without endorsing its purpose.”

“You really can make anything sound like a committee meeting.”

They reached the service court as the central pulse fell. The node was not a single machine. It was a cluster of dark columns connected by flexible, rootlike conduits that disappeared into the ground and into the walls of adjacent structures. At the base of each column, a translucent membrane expanded and contracted with the signal. Tiny points of light travelled through it in repeating sequences.

Mira crouched behind a maintenance housing and focused the optical lens. “The membrane is alive.”

“Or made from living tissue.”

“The distinction matters.”

“Not to the instrument.”

“It matters to the conclusion.”

The node emitted a faint vibration. Adrian's phase detector registered the same signature as the original navigation unit, but the pattern was richer here, with multiple frequencies locked into a single cycle. The signal moved from one column to the next, then returned through the conduits as if the structure were checking its own state.

A Veyr worker entered the court carrying a shallow container. It placed the container against the membrane and waited. The membrane brightened. A narrow filament extended from its surface and touched the worker's wrist. For a moment, the worker and node pulsed in synchrony. Then the filament withdrew, and the worker carried the container away.

Mira's eyes sharpened. “There. Direct exchange.”

“Could be maintenance authentication.”

“Could be biological regulation. The worker's tissue responded to the node before the signal completed.”

“Can you prove that from this distance?”

“No. But I can collect a sample.”

The next synchronization interval approached. They moved along the service housing until they were within reach of a conduit that had separated from the main cluster. A thin film covered its outer surface. Mira extended the sampling tool and touched the film with a sterile tip.

The detector chirped once.

The conduit pulsed.

Adrian grabbed Mira's shoulder and pulled her back behind the housing. A ripple of light passed across the node. The central columns dimmed, then brightened in sequence. For a fraction of a second, the phase detector displayed the same temporal discontinuity that had appeared in AION during the transition.

“Did it register us?” Mira whispered.

“I don't know.”

“Did we get the sample?”

She checked the cartridge. A faint residue clung to the tip. It was enough for a preliminary analysis, perhaps not enough for a full biological profile.

The service court's lights changed. A Veyr worker turned toward the conduit and placed a hand against the membrane. The pulse stabilised. The worker remained still for several seconds, then resumed its task.

Mira exhaled slowly. “It noticed a disturbance.”

“Maybe. We cannot assume it noticed us.”

“Agreed. We have our sample. We leave.”

They crossed the court during the next lull and returned to the Stillhouse without encountering another worker. The boundary-control lattice strained as they re-entered. One of the internal clocks slipped, and the toroid temperature rose sharply before Adrian's manual adjustment restored the reference state.

When the hatch sealed, neither spoke for a full minute.

Then Mira placed the sample cartridge inside the biological isolator.

The analysis took hours. The residue contained a mixture of synthetic polymers, living cells, and engineered particles similar to those found in the Veyr's repair system. The cells were not simply part of the node. They appeared to maintain a narrow timing response, exchanging phase information with the material around them.

Mira overlaid the new data with the original Veyr tissue profiles.

“The dependency is real,” she said. “Not proven to be identical across every Veyr system, but the same family of response is present.”

“Could a pathogen target it?”

“Potentially. The virus would need to bind to a Veyr-specific receptor and disrupt the synchronization pathway without relying on a single mutable surface feature. It also needs a delayed onset so infected carriers can move through the network before symptoms trigger screening.”

“Can you make that?”

“I can design a candidate. I can't promise it will work.”

Adrian looked at the transit map. “If the pathogen spreads through Veyr personnel and biological cargo, the FTL network could carry it across their colonies before the outbreak is recognised.”

“Correct. The ships are transport, not magic. Infected carriers move between systems; the network makes the journey fast enough for the infection to outrun the response.”

“And once it reaches enough nodes?”

“If the biological synchronization system is essential to the anchors, widespread disruption could destabilize the rewritten history.”

“Could.”

“Must stay a could until we have evidence.”

She saved the analysis to the isolated archive. The sample had given them the first real connection between the Veyr's biology and the temporal infrastructure. It had not yet given them a weapon.

Adrian studied the node map. “Where should the pathogen enter the network?”

“Wherever it can spread without being stopped.”

“That answers logistics, not causality. If we release it in this history, it may damage the colony without restoring the old timeline. We need to reach the network at a point where its failure can undo the rewrite.”

Mira turned from the scanner.

“Then we don't deploy it here,” she said. “We deploy it before the invasion.”

The idea changed the room. Until that moment, the pathogen had been a way to attack the Veyr's present infrastructure. Now it could become a means of preventing the temporal operation from ever succeeding.

Adrian looked at the Veyr phase signature, steady and repeating on the screen.

“If we can reach the earlier history,” he said, “we can put the virus into their network before it targets Earth.”

“And let their own transport system carry it.”

They had a direction at last. The remaining problem was the most dangerous one: whether they could reach the right moment without being lost in the process.
