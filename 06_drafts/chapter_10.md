# Chapter 10 — The Last Night of Earth

At 21:00, the world still existed.

Adrian wrote that sentence in the log because he could no longer trust a timestamp to explain what it meant. He wrote the date beneath it, then recorded the readings from all four independent clocks. Mira checked the entries without consulting the screens. They sealed the page in a metal case and placed it beside the passive reference record inside AION.

At 21:17, the command network reported that three Veyr platforms had aligned above the Indian Ocean.

At 21:22, every satellite navigation system in the region lost synchronization.

At 21:31, the national emergency broadcast played a message that had been recorded in a voice Adrian recognized as the president's. The message ordered the population to evacuate inland. Eleven minutes later, the same broadcast played a different version in which the president said there had been no evacuation order.

Mira stood at the console, watching the two recordings' metadata.

“Which one did we hear first?” she asked.

“The first one.”

“How do you know?”

“Because I wrote it down.”

She looked at the paper log. “And if the log changes?”

“Then we will have a record of the change.”

It was not enough. They both knew it.

The Veyr operation was no longer a subtle drift in old archives. It had become a pressure on the world's causal structure. Communications dropped out and returned with altered histories. People arrived at checkpoints carrying orders from commanders who denied issuing them. A medical team called to report casualties from an attack that, according to the local records, had never taken place. Their own memories did not agree with the new records, but memory was a fragile defence against a world insisting that it had always been different.

At 22:05, Captain Ilyan arrived at the calibration vault. His uniform was torn at the shoulder. He carried a hard case filled with printed command logs and a small portable clock.

“The surface command centre is gone,” he said.

“Destroyed?” Mira asked.

“I don't know what that means anymore. One team says the building was hit. Another says it was never built. The people who worked there remember different versions. The site itself is now occupied by a Veyr relay structure.”

Adrian took the hard case. “When did this happen?”

Ilyan looked at the portable clock. “According to this, tomorrow morning. According to the surviving satellite image, three days ago. According to the people who were there, it hasn't happened yet.”

He set the clock on the bench. It ticked steadily, powered by a mechanical spring.

“Why bring this to us?” Adrian asked.

“Because every electronic reference we have is being pulled into the same disagreement. This one doesn't know what time it is, but it doesn't pretend to agree with the others.”

Mira studied the little clock. “It's a useful witness.”

“That's what I thought.”

Ilyan gave them the hard case. Inside were paper maps, handwritten orders, photographs, and a list of people who had been assigned to the temporal defence effort. Several names were crossed out. Others appeared twice, with different ranks and dates of death.

“Can you stop it?” he asked.

Adrian did not lie. “No. We may be able to survive the transition. We don't know if we can stop it.”

Ilyan absorbed the answer without looking away. “Then survive. If anyone can remember what happened, it should be someone who understands the system.”

He offered no farewell. There was nothing to say that would improve the moment. He left to help evacuate the remaining staff from the lower levels, carrying the portable clock with him.

At 22:46, the first Veyr pulse reached the campus.

It did not sound like an explosion. It was a pressure change in the ears, a brief sensation of falling, and then a silence that seemed to have swallowed the hum of the building. The screens went blank. The emergency lights stayed on. The clocks stopped at different times.

Inside AION, the reference bank held.

Adrian watched the boundary trace climb and settle. Mira checked the life-support readouts. Airflow stable. Water reserve intact. Internal pressure normal. The biological monitors recorded two heart rates, both elevated.

Outside, the facility's passive sensors began returning impossible data. The campus was still there, then not there, then a structure with no human power signature. The surface temperature changed in jumps that no weather pattern could explain. A city map on the offline display revised itself, replacing roads with unfamiliar transport corridors.

Adrian opened the external camera feed. It showed the campus entrance under a sky lit by three pale bands of light. The image fragmented. For one frame, the entrance was occupied by soldiers. For the next, it was a Veyr structure built from dark ribs and smooth panels. The image stabilized on the Veyr structure.

Mira's hand found his.

“Don't look away,” she said.

“I need to record the transition.”

“You can do both.”

He turned on the passive recorder. It etched each incoming signal onto a physical storage strip, using the chamber's own clock as its reference. The process was slow and crude. It was also the only record they had that did not rely on an external database.

At 23:08, the first human archive changed.

The local cache of the national census revised its earliest records, then its recent ones. Names vanished from the display. Birth certificates became invalid references. A photograph of Adrian's parents appeared in the cache for less than a second before the file changed to an image of an empty hillside. The system did not show an error. It showed a valid record.

He reached for the screen, then stopped himself.

“It's changing the evidence,” he said.

Mira's voice was quiet. “No. It's changing what the evidence says happened.”

They watched the facility's records become unrecognizable. The research campus was no longer listed as a human institution. Its founding date changed. The underground laboratory appeared in the revised archive as a Veyr observation station established decades earlier. The names of the scientists assigned to the project disappeared one by one.

Adrian found his own name in the personnel database. It belonged to no one. The record displayed a different sequence of characters, then settled on a Veyr designation.

Mira searched for her name. The database returned no match.

She did not search again.

The external camera showed the sky darkening, though the chamber's light sensors registered no corresponding change. The Veyr platforms had aligned into a vast geometry whose points extended beyond the camera's field of view. Each pulse arrived without a visible beam. The temporal model on Adrian's screen described it as a correction to the boundary conditions of the local world.

At 23:41, the lab's external power disappeared.

AION switched to internal reserves. The toroid warmed by half a degree and stabilized. The passive reference record continued.

At 23:53, the surface sensors detected a new atmosphere over the campus. Its composition was breathable by Veyr standards and dangerous to humans over long exposure. The revised environmental archive claimed it had always been this way.

At midnight, the last reliable human broadcast ended.

The voice on the recording was not a politician or a soldier. It was a child reading a message from a school shelter. The recording cut off midway through a sentence. The next file in the sequence contained the same shelter, empty and clean, with a Veyr transit marker above the entrance.

Mira closed her eyes.

Adrian kept watching the instruments. He wanted to look away with her. He wanted to say that it would stop, that the chamber would hold, that the world outside was only temporarily lost. He had no evidence for any of those statements.

The boundary trace began to oscillate.

“Adrian,” Mira said.

“I see it.”

The toroid's phase controller was compensating for a disturbance far beyond the strength of their tests. The chamber did not resist the rewrite by force. It maintained an internal relationship between its clocks, records, and occupants, refusing to accept the external reference as the only valid sequence.

The trace dropped.

Mira reached for the manual control. “We need to reduce the boundary load.”

“If we reduce it too far, the reference may collapse.”

“If we don't, the toroid overheats.”

He studied the readings. The automatic controller was trying to follow the external phase shift, exactly the behaviour they had designed it to avoid. He switched it to manual and adjusted the ring by a fraction.

The oscillation slowed.

For one terrible second, the display showed two versions of the same timestamp. Then the internal clock held.

AION remained.

At 00:14, the transition completed—or seemed to. The external sensors stopped flickering. The camera feed stabilized. The old campus had disappeared beneath the Veyr structure. The city beyond it had become a dark, unfamiliar landscape crossed by lines of cold light.

Adrian checked the passive record. The original timeline remained inside the chamber, preserved in the clock relationships and the written logs. The world outside now described a different history.

Mira looked at the camera feed. “Is it over?”

“I think the transition has settled.”

“That wasn't my question.”

He had no answer that could comfort her. He took her hand and stood beside her while the Stillhouse listened to a world that no longer remembered the people who had built it.

Outside, the Veyr had won a war that the new history insisted had never been fought.

Inside, two humans remembered every minute.
