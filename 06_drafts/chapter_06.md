# Chapter 6 — The Signal from Yesterday

The signal was hidden in a clock.

Not the clock itself, Adrian discovered, but the calibration record stored inside its reference controller. The Veyr relay packet had arrived with a timestamp four days older than the events it described. The laboratory's six-second discontinuity had left a similar mark. And now the isolated reference unit in Lab C was recording a faint pattern that seemed to arrive before its own trigger condition.

For two nights, Adrian did not leave the facility.

Mira slept in an empty office for three hours and returned to find him surrounded by printouts, handwritten equations, and empty cups. She stood in the doorway until he noticed her.

“Have you eaten?”

“Yes.”

“What?”

He considered the question. “Food.”

“Excellent. Very specific.”

She placed a wrapped sandwich beside the keyboard. He looked at it as though it might be another instrument requiring calibration.

“I'm close,” he said.

“You said that yesterday.”

“Yesterday I was close to knowing what question to ask.”

“And today?”

“Today I think I know what the signal is doing.”

Mira pulled up a chair. “Show me.”

He brought up three traces: the Veyr relay file, the laboratory reference reset, and the new signal. Their surface details differed, but each contained a brief interval in which the ordering of events could not be explained by clock drift or ordinary transmission delay.

“Imagine the relay unit receives a message,” Adrian said. “Under normal conditions, the receiver timestamps it when it arrives. But the internal record says the message was already present before the receiving event.”

“Then either the timestamp is wrong or the message isn't arriving through the ordinary sequence.”

“Exactly. We tested the clock. We tested the storage controller. We isolated the power bus. The record survives all of it.”

“Could it be a prediction?”

“It could. But the content includes details that were not available to the relay's sensors at the time the record was written. The lab's discontinuity has no predictive content at all. It is just a reference state being restored.”

Mira studied the traces. “You think the Veyr are sending information backward.”

“I think their technology can produce records that appear to carry information from a later event into an earlier state. I can't tell you whether the information is transmitted backward, whether the device samples a future boundary condition, or whether our model of time is missing a variable.”

“That's a very long way of saying you don't know.”

“It's a very accurate way of saying I don't know.”

She nodded toward the sandwich. “Eat while you don't know.”

He obeyed, mostly because she watched until he took a bite.

The breakthrough came from the recovered navigation unit. Its internal reference crystal had been cracked by the crash, but the fracture had preserved a sequence of phase changes that the repair software had marked as corrupted. Adrian reconstructed the sequence from the surviving edges. The signal was not a message in the usual sense. It was a set of boundary conditions—values that would ordinarily be calculated after a navigation event, embedded in the record before the event occurred.

The device was not predicting a route. It was using a future state as part of the calculation that selected the route in the first place.

Mira read the reconstruction in silence.

“If this is right,” she said, “then the ship wasn't only navigating through space.”

“It was navigating through a relationship between states.”

“And the relay file?”

“May have used the same system to record future events. Or the file may be a deliberate artifact created by whoever controls the network. We need more evidence.”

She looked at the dates again. The entries were not a random list. They formed a chain: a power failure, an evacuation, a failed counterattack, a relay station's destruction. Each event influenced the next. The file contained not just events, but their dependencies.

“It's a plan,” Mira said.

“Maybe.”

“A plan written as history.”

Adrian felt the thought settle in his chest. The Veyr were not merely collecting forecasts. Their technology appeared to let them work with a future state as though it were an input. They could use later events to constrain present decisions, narrowing the range of outcomes until the desired path became the one their systems expected.

That was not the same as changing history. He wrote the distinction on the board.

**FUTURE INFORMATION IS NOT PROOF OF RETROACTIVE CONTROL.**

Mira added a second line beneath it.

**BUT IT MAY BE HOW THEY LEARN TO CONTROL IT.**

At 11:20, Captain Ilyan arrived with authorization to move the navigation unit into the laboratory's deepest shielded chamber. The military had also approved a controlled comparison with a second recovered component. The decision was less an act of trust than a recognition that the anomaly had become impossible to ignore.

They carried the unit into the chamber by hand. Its housing was heavy and cold, built from the same dark material as the fragments Mira had recovered. A ring of fine markings ran around the inner casing, too regular to be decoration and too complex to read as ordinary engineering notation.

Mira set up the sensor array. Adrian connected the isolated reference clock, then checked every link twice.

“Ready?” she asked.

“No.”

“Good. Neither am I.”

They initiated the test.

For nine seconds, nothing happened. The instruments recorded a stable baseline. Then the navigation unit emitted a pulse so weak that the first sensor classified it as noise. The second sensor caught it. The third recorded it 0.4 microseconds before the first.

Adrian stopped the run.

“Again,” Mira said.

They repeated it with the sensors reordered. The timing difference followed the reference configuration, not the physical positions of the sensors. They changed the power source, replaced the controller, and ran the test in reverse order. The anomaly persisted.

It was not a conventional transmission from one instrument to another. It was a mismatch between the order in which the system's events were recorded and the order in which the laboratory could establish that they had occurred.

Adrian felt the room narrow around him.

“It's using the reference clock as an anchor,” he said.

“An anchor to what?”

“I don't know. A state outside the local sequence. A boundary condition. Something the device treats as fixed even when our clocks disagree.”

“Can we reproduce it?”

“Not safely, and not yet.”

Mira stepped back from the array. “Then we stop here.”

He wanted one more test. He wanted to increase the pulse strength, isolate the ring markings, and find the threshold where the signal became readable. The desire was almost physical. But he remembered the way the room had tilted during the attack, and the look on Mira's face when the clocks reset.

He shut down the array.

The final data packet completed as the power bus went dark. It contained a short block of symbols translated by the military's analysis system into a partial technical phrase. The translation was uncertain, with several characters missing.

**TEMPORAL CORRECTION: REMOVE ORIGINATING SPECIES**

Mira read it twice.

“That's not a navigation instruction,” she said.

“No.”

“Could it mean removing an obstacle from the route?”

“Possibly. The translation is incomplete.”

“But if it means what it says—”

“Then the Veyr are not just using future information to win the war.”

He could not finish the sentence. He did not need to.

The phrase sat between them, clinical and monstrous. Remove originating species. Not defeat. Not occupy. Not negotiate. Remove the condition that had allowed the enemy to exist.

Mira's face was pale, but her voice remained level. “They intend to change the past so humanity never becomes a threat.”

“We have a translation fragment, not a confession.”

“Adrian.”

“I know what it looks like.”

“Then say it.”

He looked at the recovered unit. The dark casing reflected the laboratory lights without revealing its surface. The device had been designed to treat an event outside ordinary time as a usable input. The relay file had carried a chain of human events into a record dated before them. The phrase now suggested an objective that made all the earlier anomalies fit into a terrible shape.

“They may be trying to erase humanity from history,” he said.

The words were quiet. The instruments were off, yet the chamber seemed to hum around them.

Captain Ilyan took the data packet to command. Mira remained beside Adrian, staring at the phrase until the display dimmed.

“If they're changing the past,” she said, “we can't beat them by defending the present.”

“No.”

“Then we need something that can survive the change.”

Adrian looked at the reference clock, its digits steady and indifferent.

For the first time, he understood the shape of the problem. The Veyr's weapon did not need to destroy Earth's cities. It did not need to kill every human being. It only needed to alter the sequence of events that led to humanity's existence, then let the new history make the old one impossible to remember.

A victory with no battlefield.

A genocide with no corpses.

He wrote a final line on the board beneath their two hypotheses.

**IF HISTORY CAN BE REWRITTEN, WHAT CAN REMAIN OUTSIDE THE EDIT?**

Mira read it, then turned to him.

“Find out,” she said. “Before they do.”
