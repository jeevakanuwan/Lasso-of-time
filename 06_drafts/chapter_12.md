# Chapter 12 — The World Outside the Door

They waited thirty-six hours before opening the Stillhouse.

The delay was Mira's decision. Adrian wanted to leave as soon as the sensors showed a stable atmosphere and no immediate threat. Mira insisted that the chamber had been designed to survive a transition, not to guarantee that the transition was finished. They ran the environmental checks twice, tested the manual seal, and listened to the outside through a passive microphone that had no active transmitter.

The city beyond the door produced a steady wash of mechanical sounds. Transport systems. Distant ventilation. A faint harmonic pulse that repeated every forty-seven seconds.

“The same phase signature,” Adrian said.

“Then we don't touch anything until we understand what it might be doing.”

“We can't learn about the network from inside.”

“We can learn enough not to walk into a field that turns our memories into a clerical error.”

He accepted the point.

They packed a small survey kit, two sealed biological containers, a passive recorder, water, and emergency masks. Mira added the seeds from the botanical station. Adrian saw her check the packet twice and chose not to ask about it.

The outer hatch opened with a mechanical groan. Air flowed through the vestibule, passed the filters, and registered as breathable. The smell was the first thing Adrian noticed: mineral dust, warm metal, and something faintly sweet that might have been the vegetation growing along the outside wall.

He stepped onto the old campus.

The ground was still where it belonged. The concrete path had not moved, but it was split by narrow lines of pale material that pulsed in time with the distant harmonic signal. The Veyr structure had grown over the former laboratory entrance. Its surface was smooth in some places, ribbed in others, and covered with shallow geometric patterns that changed when the light moved.

No human debris remained. No broken windows, no discarded equipment, no traces of the campus's former buildings. Even the trees had changed. The old rain trees were gone, replaced by tall, slender organisms with translucent leaves and dark trunks that bent toward the nearest transport corridor.

Mira stood beside Adrian and said nothing.

He had imagined the rewritten world as a battlefield after a victorious army had passed through. It was worse than that. The Veyr had not occupied the ruins of human civilization. They had made a world in which those ruins had never existed.

They moved along the edge of the structure, keeping to the shadow of a collapsed service wall that AION's external camera had shown before the transition. The wall was no longer part of a human building. It had become a low retaining feature beside a Veyr transit lane. Adrian marked the position on their paper map, using the chamber as the only reliable origin point.

A transport passed overhead without sound. Its underside carried a series of dark modules connected by thin luminous lines. One module pulsed in the same phase pattern as the external infrastructure.

“Biological cargo?” Mira whispered.

“Could be.”

“Or a power system.”

“We won't know until we observe it more closely.”

They found the first living Veyr at a transit junction. It was taller than the captured organism from the original timeline, or perhaps the perspective was different. Its body was covered by a fitted layer of flexible material that moved with the ridges beneath its skin. It carried no visible weapon. It paused at a wall panel and pressed one hand against a recessed surface. The panel brightened, and a route appeared in the air above it.

Mira recorded the interaction through a passive optical lens.

The Veyr did not appear to be monitoring for human life. There were no patrols sweeping the area, no search lights, no visible security perimeter around the old campus. The Stillhouse had not registered as an enemy installation. It was simply an unrecognized space within a city that had no reason to expect humans.

That could change.

They returned to the chamber before the local light cycle shifted. Inside, Mira examined the images while Adrian compared the transit pulse with the old navigation unit's phase signature. The match was not exact, but the underlying pattern was unmistakable.

“The network isn't just in orbit,” he said. “It runs through the infrastructure.”

“Distributed anchors,” Mira said. “A city-scale system, maybe larger.”

“Or the city is one node in a wider network.”

She opened the image of the transport's underside. The dark modules had the same repeating geometry as the material recovered from the crashed Veyr craft before the timeline changed.

“If the system depends on biological coordination,” she said, “we may be able to interfere with it through the pathogen.”

“We still don't know whether the biological dependency is part of the anchor network or just a feature of the species.”

“Then we need a sample from a living Veyr.”

“Without alerting the city.”

“Exactly.”

The next survey took them farther from the old campus, to a maintenance area where the transit lane entered a broad structure built around a pulsing central column. They observed workers moving sealed containers from a vehicle into a receiving bay. The containers were marked with a repeating symbol that resembled the Veyr protein pattern Mira had isolated in the original timeline.

She zoomed the image. “That symbol could be a cargo category.”

“Or a warning.”

“Or a routing marker. We don't have a translation.”

One of the containers opened briefly during transfer. A Veyr worker placed a hand against its interior surface. A faint pulse moved through the worker's arm and into the container's lining. The worker paused, then continued the transfer.

Mira lowered the recorder. “There. Did you see the response?”

“Yes.”

“The worker and the container exchanged a signal.”

“Possibly.”

She gave him a warning look.

He amended the sentence. “The recording shows a response travelling from the worker's contact point into the container lining. We need a better view before we decide what it means.”

“Thank you.”

They returned with no sample and no proof, only observations. It was less than either of them wanted, but more than they had when they opened the door.

That night, Mira began a new research plan. She would not design a pathogen from the old data alone. The Veyr in this history might differ biologically from the Veyr they had studied before the transition. Their evolution had followed a different chain of events, and the technology that maintained the rewritten timeline might have altered the relationship between organism and infrastructure.

“We need to establish continuity,” she said. “Same species, same dependency, same response. If we assume the old data transfers perfectly, we could miss a change that makes the pathogen useless.”

Adrian reviewed the network recordings. “And I need to identify the anchor synchronization pattern. If the pathogen works, it has to disrupt something that matters to the timeline, not just make the Veyr ill.”

Mira looked at him. “Then we're not building a disease. We're trying to find the system's point of failure.”

“That's a less alarming description.”

“It's also more accurate.”

They set the first task for the next survey: obtain a tiny, nonlethal biological trace from a Veyr transit worker or from a surface recently touched by one, without exposing themselves or the Stillhouse. The second task was to map the pulse from the transit infrastructure and compare it with the original navigation record.

The work would be slow. They had no laboratory staff, no supply chain, and no assurance that the Stillhouse would remain hidden. Every excursion risked revealing that the rewritten world contained two people it had deleted.

Mira placed the seed packet on the shelf beside the research notes.

“One day,” she said, “we should plant these.”

Adrian looked at the sealed chamber, the alien city, and the little packet of seeds.

“One day,” he agreed.

They closed the hatch and returned to work.

A minute later, the passive recorder chimed. It had logged the next synchronization pulse forty-seven seconds before the signal itself arrived.

Mira read the trace twice. “Either the node is changing its own record,” she said, “or it knows what it's about to do.”
