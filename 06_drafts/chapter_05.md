# Chapter 5 — The First Kiss During the Sirens

The attack began at 16:03, while Mira was explaining why the word *weapon* had become useless.

“It's a tool with an intended outcome,” she said, marking a protein cluster on the display. “A weapon is a tool intended to cause harm. What we're doing is trying to understand a dependency. If it becomes a weapon, that decision should not be made by the language we use in the lab.”

Adrian had one hand on the console and the other wrapped around a mug that had been reheated so many times the coffee had become a separate chemical category.

“Command won't make that distinction.”

“Then command can use a different room.”

The first impact cut off the rest of the argument.

The lights vanished. A hard, metallic groan rolled through the walls, followed by the sound of ventilation dampers slamming shut. Emergency strips glowed along the floor. The laboratory's outer door locked with a heavy clunk.

“Status,” Adrian said.

The ceiling speaker clicked, hissed, and produced half a sentence: “—breach reported in the upper transit—”

A second impact shook the lab. The specimen case slid several centimetres across the bench. Mira caught it before it reached the edge.

“Containment,” she said.

“Stable,” Adrian answered, reading the local sensors. “The chamber's still sealed.”

A pressure alarm sounded from the corridor. The doors between Lab C and the central shelter had closed automatically, leaving them isolated behind the reinforced research barrier. Adrian pulled up the emergency map. The display showed a red zone spreading across the upper levels.

“There's a fire between us and the interior stairwell,” he said. “The secondary route is blocked.”

“How long until the suppression system clears it?”

“If the doors hold, ten minutes. If they don't, we won't be worrying about the route.”

Mira looked at him. “You have a gift for reassurance.”

“I've been told.”

The laboratory shifted again, a low lateral shudder that made the instrument arms rattle. Somewhere beyond the wall, a heavy object fell and kept falling, clanging down a shaft until the sound stopped.

Adrian opened the manual emergency panel. The isolation system had severed external power, but the battery bus remained live. He could force the inner door to cycle, yet doing so would open the corridor to whatever pressure differential the damaged ventilation system had created.

“Don't,” Mira said.

“I haven't done anything.”

“You're about to try to outthink a fire door.”

“The panel gives us a manual override.”

“And the pressure sensor says the corridor is unsafe.”

“If the sensor is damaged—”

“Then we verify the sensor. We don't gamble because standing still makes you feel helpless.”

He stared at her. The warning tone kept sounding, steady and irritating. He checked the redundant pressure gauge. It agreed with the first. The corridor was not safe.

He lowered his hand from the panel.

“Thank you,” he said.

Mira blinked. “For what?”

“Stopping me from doing something stupid.”

“I'll add it to my annual performance review.”

The lights flickered. On the main monitor, the pathogen model froze in the middle of a cycle. Then the laboratory clocks slipped out of agreement. One recorded 16:04:12. The second read 16:04:11. The third displayed a timestamp that had not yet occurred.

Adrian forgot the door.

“That's the same signature.”

Mira came beside him. “Can we save the data?”

“Local buffer only. The reference bus is unstable.”

“Then save the buffer.”

He initiated a dump to the isolated drive. The progress bar stopped at eighty-three percent. A static line crawled across the monitor.

For a few seconds, the room seemed to tilt—not physically, but in the way a familiar place can feel wrong when one detail has moved by a fraction. Adrian's inner ear insisted he was still. His eyes insisted the hanging instruments had shifted. The clocks disagreed about how long he had been staring.

Mira took his wrist.

“Adrian. Look at me.”

He did.

“Tell me five things you can see.”

“What?”

“Five things.”

He swallowed. “The console. The specimen case. Your hand. The emergency light. The pressure gauge.”

“Four things you can feel.”

“The floor. The bench edge. My sleeve. Your hand.”

“Good. Stay here.”

The sensation passed. The clocks snapped back into agreement. The data dump resumed and completed.

Adrian realized that Mira was still holding his wrist. He realized, too, that he had closed his fingers around hers.

Neither moved.

The emergency speaker came alive with a burst of static. A voice announced casualties in the upper transit corridor, then broke into unintelligible noise. The words were distant and clinical. They did not make the deaths feel smaller.

Mira released his hand, but only enough to look at him properly.

“You're shaking,” she said.

“So are you.”

“I know.”

They stood close together in the emergency light. He could see a small cut near her hairline, bright with fresh blood. He reached for the first-aid kit, but she caught his hand.

“It's not serious.”

“Let me see.”

“Adrian—”

“Please.”

The word surprised them both. Mira went quiet. He cleaned the cut with a sterile wipe and sealed it with a narrow dressing. His fingers were steady now, but he was aware of every place they touched: her temple, the edge of her cheek, the warm pulse in her wrist when she lowered her hand.

“You don't have to turn every fear into a procedure,” she said softly.

“I don't know what else to do with it.”

“Sometimes you can just say you're afraid.”

He looked at her. Beyond the walls, the sirens rose and fell. The laboratory had become a small, sealed island in a world of smoke and collapsing infrastructure.

“I'm afraid,” he said.

Mira's expression changed. She did not smile or reassure him. She simply stepped closer.

“Me too.”

He had imagined kissing her many times, though never with the clarity of an actual plan. In those imagined versions, there had been time, privacy, and some certainty that the world would not interrupt. Now there was a sealed door, a failing clock system, and a casualty report repeating from a damaged speaker.

He could have waited for a better moment. He had spent months waiting for better moments.

“Mira,” he began.

“I know.”

“I don't think you do.”

“Then tell me.”

He tried. The words assembled and fell apart. Every honest sentence seemed too small beside the war, and every grand one sounded like a lie.

So he stopped trying to explain and asked, “Can I kiss you?”

Her answer was almost lost beneath the siren.

“Yes.”

The kiss was brief at first, cautious in the way of two people who had spent so long keeping one part of themselves under control that they had forgotten how to begin. Then Mira's hand rose to the side of his face, and he felt the last of his practiced distance give way.

It was not a promise that they would survive. It was not a victory. It was one moment in which neither of them had to pretend that the other was merely a colleague standing nearby.

When they parted, Mira rested her forehead against his.

“That,” she said, breathless, “was not in the research plan.”

“I'll update the documentation.”

“Don't you dare.”

He laughed once, quietly. She did too. The sound felt fragile and astonishing.

Then the clocks chimed.

Not the usual hourly signal. A single note rang through the laboratory's speakers, clean and cold. Every display changed at once. The casualty report vanished. The pathogen model returned to its first frame. The emergency timer reset to 16:03:00.

Adrian pulled away and stared at the console.

“That's impossible.”

Mira reached for the keyboard. “What reset?”

“Everything tied to the local reference bus.”

“Did we lose the buffer?”

He checked the drive. The data remained. The system logs showed a six-second discontinuity, followed by a synchronisation event whose timestamp predated the impact.

The alarm continued, but the wall display showed a status message unlike any he had seen:

**REFERENCE STATE RESTORED — EXTERNAL CAUSALITY UNVERIFIED**

Mira's fingers tightened on the bench edge.

“Is that a Veyr message?”

“I don't know.”

“Could the platform have caused it?”

“Maybe. Or the attack exposed a weakness in their system. Or this is a local failure that only resembles the earlier events.”

“Three possibilities. No conclusion.”

He looked at her. “You're learning my habits.”

“Someone has to keep them from becoming a religion.”

The outer door remained locked. The pressure alarm still indicated danger, and the casualty report returned in fragments. Nothing about the world had become safer.

But the six-second discontinuity had left a trace. The system had not merely lost time. It had restored a reference state, as if some hidden process had briefly tried to put the laboratory back the way it had been.

Adrian copied the new logs.

Mira took his hand again, deliberately this time.

“Whatever that was,” she said, “we face it together.”

He wanted to answer with a theory. Instead, he held her hand and stayed beside her until the rescue team cut through the outer access panel.

The war had taken six seconds from the room.

It had not taken the moment they had chosen to keep.
