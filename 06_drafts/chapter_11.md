# Chapter 11 — Causal Collapse

The world outside the Stillhouse did not end.

It continued.

That was the first cruelty Adrian understood when the sensors stabilised. There had been no final flash, no universal silence, no instant in which the planet ceased to be. The Veyr had not destroyed Earth. They had changed the history that made Earth human.

The camera feed showed a structure rising from the old campus, its dark ribs curving over the ground like the skeleton of an enormous seed. Pale transport lights moved along tracks that had not existed the day before. Beyond it, the city was unfamiliar but not ruined. Towers stood where human buildings had once crowded the horizon, though their shapes were different and their surfaces reflected the sky in muted bands of green and silver.

AION's sensors found air, water, gravity, and a stable planetary magnetic field. The environment was still Earth. The history was not.

Mira sat on the floor beside the life-support rack, arms wrapped around her knees. Adrian had offered her water, then food, then the option of sleeping. She had accepted the water and refused the rest.

“Show me the records,” she said.

He brought up the passive archive.

They had preserved the original timeline as a set of local measurements: handwritten logs, clock relationships, photographs, isolated drives, and the physical record strip. None of those things could prove every detail of the lost world. But together they formed a chain of evidence that the world outside now denied.

The last external archive had been replaced. The campus's history described a Veyr research station that had existed for eighty-three local years. Human civilization was absent from the records. The species database listed no terrestrial hominin line. The city map had no streets, districts, or monuments that Adrian recognised. The planetary environment had been shaped around Veyr settlement and transport.

Mira searched for the names of their colleagues.

Nothing.

She searched for her parents.

Nothing.

Adrian watched her fingers hover over the keyboard, then fall to her lap.

“The absence is consistent,” he said, hating himself as soon as he heard the words.

“Don't turn it into a result.”

He sat beside her. “I'm sorry.”

“I don't need you to explain it.”

“I know.”

They listened to the ventilation system. Inside the chamber, every fan and pump had a sound that had become familiar during construction. The water recycler clicked at the end of its cycle. The air monitor gave a soft double tone. Those small mechanical sounds were evidence that they were still alive in a place built to keep them that way.

Mira opened the last handwritten log from before the transition. The page contained two lines in her writing:

*We are afraid.*

*We are still here.*

She pressed her thumb to the paper.

“Do you think anyone else survived?” she asked.

“Not inside a causal boundary like this one.”

“That isn't the same as no.”

“No. It isn't.”

They checked the global sensors. AION had only limited external reach; its instruments had been designed to monitor local conditions, not survey a planet. Still, the passive receiver picked up traffic patterns, energy signatures, and repeated signals from Veyr infrastructure. No human transmissions appeared. No anomalous local reference state matched theirs. The planet seemed to operate according to the new history.

At 03:20, Adrian noticed a discrepancy in the external atmospheric model. The Veyr structures were emitting a narrow band of radiation that did not fit the facility's own revised environmental baseline. The signature resembled the phase pattern recorded during the transition.

“The network is still active,” he said.

Mira came to the display. “The temporal anchors?”

“Possibly. Whatever the Veyr used to establish this history, it isn't just a one-time pulse. The infrastructure is maintaining the new state.”

“Can we disrupt it?”

“Not from here. We don't even know where the anchors are.”

“Then we learn.”

They made a list of what they knew and what they did not. They knew AION had preserved them through the transition. They knew the outside history now denied humanity's existence. They knew the Veyr network continued to emit the same phase signature. They did not know whether the new history was permanent, whether other local boundaries existed, or whether the Veyr could eventually detect the Stillhouse.

The last question was the one Adrian could not ignore. The chamber had remained invisible to the external reference system, but invisibility was not the same as safety. The Veyr might never notice the anomaly, or they might find it the moment they performed a sufficiently detailed survey of the old campus.

Mira read the list.

“We have to leave eventually,” she said.

“Yes.”

“Not today.”

“No.”

She rested her head against the wall. For the first time since the transition, she looked exhausted enough to sleep. Adrian arranged a blanket beside her, then sat at the console and watched the external feed.

The Veyr transit lights moved through the city with calm regularity. Their world was not a battlefield. It was a functioning civilization built on a history in which humanity had never become a problem.

Adrian tried to hate them. It would have been easier if the city looked like a ruin, if the Veyr had left the planet poisoned or dead. Instead, they had built a life there. They had erased one species to secure their own future, and the result looked ordinary from the outside.

At 05:00, Mira woke and found him watching the city.

“What are you thinking?” she asked.

“That the world has no reason to know what happened.”

She sat beside him.

“Then we remember it,” she said.

“And we find a way to make the change reversible.”

She looked at the phase signature on the screen. “The network is holding the history together.”

“If we can disrupt its synchronisation, perhaps the imposed branch won't remain stable.”

“Perhaps.”

“It may be the only way.”

Mira's gaze moved to the sealed biological samples. The pathogen project had been a research question before the transition. Now it was their only possible route back to the world they had lost.

“We need a Veyr biological sample that tells us what the anchors depend on,” she said.

Adrian nodded.

The Stillhouse had preserved two witnesses. It had not preserved a civilization, an army, or even a reliable map. It had preserved enough evidence for them to know that history had been changed—and enough of their work to begin asking how to change it back.

Outside, the Veyr world continued without a single memory of the species it had replaced.
