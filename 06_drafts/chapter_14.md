# Chapter 14 — Ghosts in the Laboratory

By the second month, the Stillhouse had developed the rhythms of a small, difficult household.

The air recycler needed a filter change every eleven days. The water system made a clicking noise when its mineral cartridge began to clog. The toroid's cooling pump vibrated at a frequency Adrian could recognize from the other side of the room. Mira had labelled the storage drawers with the names of ordinary things rather than inventory codes: *food*, *clean clothes*, *medical*, *things we hope not to need*.

The last drawer contained spare fuses, a manual seal kit, and a folded photograph of the two of them taken before the transition. The image had been printed from an old lab camera. In it, Mira was laughing at something outside the frame while Adrian looked at her instead of the camera.

They had not discussed the photograph. They did not need to.

Isolation changed them in ways neither could measure. Adrian began to speak aloud while working, narrating his calculations as if an invisible colleague might catch a mistake. Mira started keeping a daily list of things she could smell, hear, or touch beyond the lab: wet stone, hot dust, the vibration of a transport, the rough bark of a Veyr-grown tree. The lists were not scientific records. She kept them anyway.

They argued about the survey schedule, the risk of detection, the limits of their food reserves, and whether Adrian's insistence on calibrating every instrument before breakfast was a symptom of a deeper disorder.

“It is not a disorder,” he said.

“It's a ritual.”

“Rituals are repeatable procedures.”

“That's not a defence.”

He smiled, but she did not. The argument had started over a minor fault in the external receiver and drifted into the larger question neither wanted to ask: how long could two people remain inside a room while the world outside lived without them?

The answer was not in the life-support estimates. AION could keep them physically alive for months, perhaps longer with strict rationing. But the boundary-control system consumed more power than the original design predicted. Every external survey forced the chamber to re-establish its causal reference. The toroid had survived the transition, but the control lattice was slowly accumulating stress.

Adrian showed Mira the latest maintenance forecast.

“How many more excursions?” she asked.

“At the current rate, one safe mission. Maybe two if the field stays stable.”

“Maybe?”

“I won't promise a number the data can't support.”

She looked at him with the same expression she had used before the first test. “Then we need to decide what the last mission is for.”

They had spent weeks collecting observations, mapping transit nodes, and comparing the Veyr biological signals with the temporal network. The evidence now suggested that the anchor infrastructure was not maintained by machines alone. Living organisms, or biological systems derived from them, appeared to regulate the phase relationship between nodes. The Veyr's technology and biology were entangled in a way that made the civilization efficient—and potentially vulnerable.

But the samples they had gathered were only surface traces. They had not obtained a living tissue specimen from a current Veyr organism. They had no way to test whether a pathogen could interrupt the coordination network without killing indiscriminately. They also lacked a complete map of the temporal anchors.

“We need one living sample and a better view of the central node,” Mira said. “If the biological response is the same as in the original timeline, I can narrow the pathogen target. If it's different, we stop and rethink.”

“And if we find the central node?”

“We learn whether it is a relay, a regulator, or the anchor itself.”

“And if the field destabilizes while we're outside?”

“Then we return to the Stillhouse.”

“Unless the return route is affected.”

Mira held his gaze. “There is no zero-risk option left.”

He hated that she was right. He hated more that the choice was not between danger and safety, but between a risk they could describe and a future in which the Veyr's rewritten history became permanent.

That evening, they reviewed the chamber's remaining reserves. Water was sufficient. Food could be stretched. The air system was stable. The boundary-control lattice was not. AION could probably survive one more major phase disturbance, but the second might fracture the superconducting ring beyond repair.

Mira placed the packet of seeds on the table between them.

“I want us to plant these before we go,” she said.

“Why?”

“Because if we come back, I want one thing outside this room that isn't a Veyr system.”

“Plants need soil, water, and time.”

“Yes, Adrian. That's why they're plants.”

They found a narrow patch of ground behind the Stillhouse, where the Veyr's new vegetation had not covered the old retaining wall. Mira pressed the seeds into the soil while Adrian held a small water container. They marked the spot with a piece of inert metal that could not transmit a signal.

The seeds might never germinate. The soil might not support them. The Veyr's environmental systems might remove the patch during routine maintenance. It was an act without a reliable outcome.

Mira brushed the dirt from her hands.

“There,” she said.

“What does it prove?”

“Nothing.”

He waited.

“It means we did something that wasn't about surviving the next hour.”

He understood then that the Stillhouse was becoming a trap in more ways than one. Its systems protected their bodies and memories, but the room could also shrink their world until every choice became a calculation about how to remain inside it. They could survive indefinitely and still allow the Veyr to win by never leaving.

Adrian closed the maintenance forecast.

“One mission,” he said. “We get the sample, map the central node, and come back.”

Mira nodded. “And then we decide whether the virus can reach the network.”

They returned to the lab. The external receiver showed the central pulse repeating across the city, steady as a heartbeat.

The Stillhouse had one more journey left to spend.

They needed to make it count.
