# Chapter 4 — The Shape of a Human Future

The first thing Mira asked for after the intelligence briefing was a window.

The command facility had none. It had screens showing weather, satellite feeds, evacuation corridors, and the shifting boundaries of the war. Mira wanted actual glass, actual daylight, and a view that had not been filtered through a military system.

Adrian found a maintenance balcony above the old campus's eastern retaining wall. The balcony had been built for ventilation engineers, not scientists, and its railing vibrated whenever the emergency generators changed load. Beyond the concrete barriers, the sky was pale with smoke. Farther east, the clouds over the sea carried a thin orange line.

“It's not a very good view,” Adrian said.

“It isn't trying to sell me anything.”

They stood with their hands on the cold railing. For several minutes, neither spoke. The last briefing had confirmed that the Veyr possessed records of events before they occurred—or something close enough to make the distinction frightening. Command had ordered every research group to submit plans for “strategic denial of future hostile capability,” a phrase no one in the room had been willing to define.

Mira watched a distant aircraft turn back toward the coast.

“Do you think they can see the future?” she asked.

“No.”

She glanced at him.

“I think they have a system that produces information which resembles future knowledge,” he said. “That could mean prediction at a scale we can't match. It could mean a causal effect. It could mean their clocks are lying in a way we don't understand.”

“You have a talent for making terrifying things sound like paperwork.”

“Paperwork is terrifying.”

“Not to the people who have to bury the dead.”

He accepted the rebuke. In the last months, Mira had become less patient with abstraction when it offered no path back to the people abstraction had erased. Adrian admired that about her. He also feared it. He could spend a night with an equation and call it progress. Mira had to look at what the equation would do to a living thing.

“What would you do,” she asked, “if we won?”

He turned from the sea. “Win how?”

“Adrian.”

“I know. I'm avoiding the question.”

“Yes.”

He thought about it. The honest answer was embarrassingly small. He wanted to sleep for a week, eat food that did not come in sealed packets, and wake without wondering whether the building would still exist. He wanted the clocks to mean what they said. He wanted to see a city at night with all its lights on.

“I'd go somewhere quiet,” he said. “Somewhere I didn't have to measure anything.”

“You would last three days.”

“Four.”

“You'd start measuring the wind.”

“Wind is measurable.”

She laughed, and the sound startled him. It had been too long since he had heard laughter without a nervous edge.

“What about you?” he asked.

“I'd plant something.”

“Anything in particular?”

“Something difficult. A fruit tree, maybe. Something that takes years before it gives you anything back.”

“That's an inefficient use of land.”

“That's why I want to do it.”

He imagined a garden with no perimeter fence, no air-raid sirens, no warning lights. He imagined Mira arguing with a tree because it refused to grow according to schedule. He imagined being there long enough to see the first fruit.

The thought hurt because it felt possible.

“Maybe we could find a place together,” he said.

The words came out more easily than he had expected. He had not planned to say them. He had not planned anything beyond the next experiment for so long that a sentence about the future felt like an unauthorized system call.

Mira looked at him. The smoke in the sky shifted, briefly letting a band of sunlight reach the balcony.

“Together?” she asked.

“If you want.”

“Is that a scientific proposal or a personal one?”

“I haven't written the methodology.”

“Then I can't approve it.”

He looked away, embarrassed by how much the answer mattered. “I meant it as a personal one.”

Mira rested her elbows on the railing. “I want a place with rain. Real rain, not whatever falls through the filters at the shelters. I want a kitchen where the lights don't flicker. I want to grow something and not have to label it as a strategic resource.”

“Those are all achievable objectives.”

“And I want to stop being afraid that every conversation is the last one we'll have.”

He had no equation for that. He moved his hand along the railing until it rested near hers. She closed the gap herself.

They did not kiss. Not then. The promise was quieter than that, and more dangerous: for a few minutes, they allowed themselves to imagine that victory would leave them with a life to choose.

The alert arrived before they returned to the lab.

A Veyr research platform had been detected above the northern hemisphere, outside the range of conventional weapons. It had not fired. It had not maneuvered toward a defence target. It had simply altered its orbit to maintain a line of observation over several human scientific facilities, including their own.

Command classified it as surveillance. The public broadcast called it a precursor to an attack.

Adrian studied the orbital solution. “It's watching our research sites.”

“Maybe it watches anything that generates an unusual signal,” Mira said.

“The phase anomalies?”

“Or the pathogen work.”

They had been careful. The project ran on isolated instruments, and its records were held inside a segmented network. But the Veyr had already demonstrated a capacity to obtain information from damaged equipment and anomalous reference signals. Security was a problem they could not solve by adding another password.

At 14:30, Captain Ilyan arrived with a new directive. The Veyr platform had transmitted a narrow-band pulse toward the research corridor. The pulse did not damage equipment. It caused every instrument using the laboratory's primary reference clock to lose synchronisation for 0.6 seconds.

Mira stared at the trace. “It wasn't a weapon.”

“No,” Adrian said. “It was a measurement.”

“Of what?”

He compared the pulse signature with the black fragments' response and the timestamp from the Veyr relay file. The same phase reversal appeared in all three.

“They may be testing whether our facility can observe the anomaly.”

“Or whether we can reproduce it,” Mira said.

A new alert appeared on Captain Ilyan's tablet. The laboratory security system had detected an attempted access to the pathogen project's metadata. No files had been opened, and the intrusion had not come through the ordinary network. It had arrived as a malformed synchronisation request sent to a reference controller that was supposed to be physically isolated.

The request contained a string of characters that the translation system rendered as a Veyr technical label.

**BIOLOGICAL COUNTERMEASURE — HUMAN ORIGIN**

Mira's face lost its colour.

“They know,” Ilyan said.

“They know a project exists,” Adrian replied. “They may not know what it does.”

“Does that distinction help us?”

“It tells us what we should assume they can see.”

Mira pulled the pathogen project offline. “We need to move the work.”

“Where?” Adrian asked. “Every facility with a secure lab is already on their list.”

“Then we make the work harder to observe.”

Captain Ilyan looked between them. “Can you continue?”

Mira answered carefully. “We can continue studying the vulnerability. We are not ready to build a deployable pathogen, and we are not going to pretend otherwise.”

The captain nodded, but the expression in his eyes had changed. They had started with a possible biological weakness. Now the enemy had identified their work before they had even decided what to call it.

That evening, Adrian returned to the maintenance balcony alone. The sky was dark, and the Veyr platform was invisible to the naked eye. On the horizon, distant lights flickered in the direction of the port.

He tried to imagine the future they had discussed. A garden. Rain. A kitchen. Mira complaining about a tree. A life made from ordinary days rather than emergency intervals.

He could not tell whether the image was a promise or a superstition.

His tablet vibrated. Mira had sent him a message from the lab.

**I found a second reference pattern in the countermeasure request. It matches the relay file. Come now.**

Adrian looked once more at the sky and went inside.

The Veyr had seen enough of their work to name it. The next question was whether they were trying to stop a human weapon—or whether the weapon had become important because it could interfere with something the Veyr had already set in motion.

For the first time, Adrian understood that the war might not be a contest over territory, resources, or even survival.

It might be a contest over which future was allowed to exist.
