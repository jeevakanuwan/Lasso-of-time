# Chapter 9 — Building the Stillhouse

AION began as a room-sized argument.

The superconducting toroid arrived from a particle research facility that had lost half its staff and all confidence in the national grid. The atomic clock bank came from a navigation laboratory whose director signed the transfer papers with a hand that would not stop shaking. The metamaterial panels were originally intended for electromagnetic shielding. The phase-control assembly was assembled from three experimental systems that had never been designed to work together.

None of the components had been built for time.

Adrian insisted on recording that fact in the project log.

Mira read the line and added: *Nor have they been tested against a universe that disagrees with itself.*

“Your contribution is not helping morale,” he said.

“Your morale was based on a false premise.”

They built the chamber in an old calibration vault beneath the research campus. The vault had thick walls, independent power, and a floor designed to carry equipment heavier than a small house. Its ventilation could be sealed without cutting off emergency oxygen. The door was a salvaged pressure hatch, reinforced with a second layer of composite material and fitted with mechanical locks that did not rely on network authorization.

The shell looked less like a science-fiction machine than a bunker assembled by people who had run out of time. Inside, the toroid surrounded a central platform. Clock modules lined one wall. The control console sat behind a physical barrier, with paper switches and manual interlocks added wherever the electronics could be bypassed.

Mira supervised the biological systems. The chamber needed to keep two people alive during a transition of unknown duration. Air, water, waste recycling, temperature control, and emergency medical supplies were all limited by volume. She designed redundancies, then designed procedures for when the redundancies failed.

“What's the expected duration?” a technician asked.

“Unknown,” Mira replied.

“Days?”

“Possibly.”

“Weeks?”

“Possibly.”

“Years?”

Mira paused. “If the outside timeline changes and doesn't return, we have no basis for estimating how long we'll be inside.”

The technician looked at the life-support rack.

“Then we build for the longest duration we can support,” she said, “and we plan for the possibility that it won't be enough.”

Adrian's work was more abstract. The chamber's clocks would not simply agree with one another. They would maintain a local reference state and continuously record their relationships. The toroid would stabilize the timing architecture against external phase disturbances. The shell would reduce ordinary electromagnetic coupling. The boundary-control system—still speculative—would use the anomaly's own mathematical signature to define the chamber as a causally isolated domain.

He had no proof that such a boundary could exist. He had evidence that the Veyr technology behaved as if it depended on one.

The first test used no biological occupants. They placed a rack of independent clocks inside the chamber, sealed the hatch, and initiated a weak phase pulse from the recovered navigation unit. Outside the chamber, the clocks disagreed by 0.3 microseconds. Inside, the reference bank held its phase relationship.

For seven seconds.

Then one clock slipped.

The boundary collapsed before the safety interlock could shut the system down. A fuse blew. The toroid rang with a sound like a struck bell. The chamber lights went out, and a ceramic panel cracked across its centre.

Nobody was hurt. The test was a failure.

Adrian stared at the trace. “The field amplitude was too high.”

“Or the boundary geometry was wrong,” Mira said.

“Or the phase controller is feeding back on itself.”

“Or the premise is wrong.”

He turned to her.

“You said you wanted careful science,” she said. “That includes the possibility that we are building an expensive room around a mistake.”

“I know.”

“Do you?”

He looked at the damaged panel, then at the clock traces. “Yes.”

They spent the next day rebuilding the boundary controller. They changed the toroid's operating sequence, isolated the clock bank from the main control bus, and added a passive reference record that could not be overwritten by the system software. Mira reduced the number of life-support interfaces connected to the central console and added manual controls to the air and water systems.

On the second test, the chamber held for nineteen seconds.

On the third, it held for four minutes.

On the fourth, the Veyr pulse from the navigation unit changed frequency halfway through the test. Every external clock jumped. Inside AION, the clocks shifted by a fraction, then returned to their original relationship. The passive record preserved the discontinuity rather than smoothing it away.

Adrian leaned against the console, breathing hard.

Mira watched the output. “Did it work?”

“It held a local reference state through an external disturbance.”

“That sounds like a yes with qualifications.”

“It's a yes with very large qualifications.”

“Good. I distrust unqualified yeses.”

They began the fifth test with a stronger pulse. The boundary remained stable for eleven minutes. At the end of the run, the chamber's internal clock recorded a short interval that had no corresponding duration in the external reference. It was not time travel. No object had moved into the past. But the local record remained self-consistent while the external clocks disagreed about the sequence.

Adrian printed the trace and taped it to the wall.

The machine had not stopped time. It had not shielded them from every possible effect. It had preserved an observer's local causal reference through an anomaly that should have destabilized it.

Mira stood beside him, studying the curve.

“A cage keeps things out,” she said.

Adrian looked at the chamber.

“This keeps history out.”

She turned to him. “Put that in the documentation. It's the first useful sentence you've said about the design.”

“It's not technically precise.”

“It's humanly precise.”

For the first time in days, they allowed themselves a few minutes away from the machine. They ate from sealed ration packs on the calibration vault floor, their backs against the wall, surrounded by cable spools and spare panels. Mira leaned her head against his shoulder.

“If it works,” she said, “we might wake up in a world where none of this happened.”

“If the boundary holds.”

“And if it doesn't?”

“Then we will have built a very expensive room.”

She laughed softly. “I knew there was a reason I liked you.”

He turned toward her. “Because of my engineering pessimism?”

“Because when you're terrified, you still try to make the truth fit in a sentence.”

He kissed her hair. The gesture was so ordinary that it frightened him more than the tests had.

A new report arrived from command. The temporal correction window was expanding. The Veyr had begun synchronizing multiple orbital platforms, and the anomaly now affected archival systems across every continent. The command network could no longer guarantee that its orders remained in the same historical sequence long enough to be executed.

AION's final test had to happen soon.

Mira stood, brushing dust from her trousers. “We need to stock the chamber.”

“We need one more test.”

“We've done enough to establish the boundary under controlled conditions. The next test risks the equipment we need to survive.”

Adrian knew she was right. He hated that being right had become such a costly thing.

He shut down the pulse source.

They loaded water, nutrient packs, medical supplies, spare clothing, printed records, and the isolated drives. Mira added a small packet of seeds from the botanical station's emergency stores. Adrian noticed but did not comment.

“What?” she asked.

“Nothing.”

“You're looking at the seeds.”

“I was calculating their storage life.”

“Of course you were.”

He helped her pack them carefully.

AION was not a promise that they would survive. It was a way to give survival a chance, and to preserve the truth if the rest of the world lost the ability to remember it.

Outside the vault, the clocks began to disagree again.
