# Chapter 16 — The Virus

Mira named the candidate LANTERN because she wanted a word that meant guidance rather than death.

Adrian said it sounded like a project that would eventually be renamed by a committee. Mira replied that if they survived long enough for a committee to rename it, the name would have served its purpose.

The work took nine days.

She built the candidate from the biological evidence they had gathered: the original Veyr tissue data preserved inside AION, the current colony sample, and the timing response measured at the central node. The design depended on a combination of Veyr-specific molecular recognition and a disruption pathway that would not activate in ordinary terrestrial cells. It was not intended to destroy every cell it encountered. Its intended effect was to interfere with the Veyr's synchronised repair and signalling system, leaving the organism increasingly unable to coordinate the processes on which its advanced biology depended.

The candidate remained a model until the final day. Mira tested its predicted binding against both sets of Veyr data, then tested the model against a library of human molecular structures. It showed no obvious route to binding human cells, but the library was incomplete and the model could not prove universal safety. She recorded the uncertainty in the first line of the report.

Adrian read it.

“You're leading with the risk.”

“It's the first thing anyone should know.”

“Command would have hated that.”

“Command isn't here.”

The Stillhouse was silent except for the air system and the cooling pump. Their remaining supplies had been reduced to a careful schedule. The boundary lattice was stable at low load, but the last external mission had left a permanent shift in one superconducting ring. Adrian could compensate for it, not repair it. The chamber could survive another transition if the field behaved within the model. It could not survive an uncontrolled failure.

Mira finished the first viable candidate and sealed it inside a triple containment capsule. It was not a weapon ready for release. It was a tightly controlled research sample whose intended use depended on a chain of events they had not yet achieved: temporal transit to the earlier history, delivery into a Veyr biological carrier, and spread through the civilization's FTL logistics network before the Veyr recognised the outbreak.

“If we deploy it in the wrong place,” she said, “it may kill organisms and change nothing about the timeline.”

“If we deploy it too early, the Veyr might detect it and develop a countermeasure.”

“If we deploy it too late, the anchor network may already have stabilised the rewritten branch.”

Adrian opened the temporal model. “We need the point at which their network is vulnerable but the invasion hasn't begun.”

Mira sat beside him. “Can the model identify that?”

“It can narrow the interval. It can't tell us what happens if we intervene.”

“Then we have to make the best choice we can.”

He was not ready to say that. For years, he had treated uncertainty as a temporary condition that more data could remove. Now the only path forward required an irreversible decision based on incomplete knowledge.

He studied the recovered Veyr temporal device. Its navigation system had survived in pieces, and the Stillhouse had preserved enough of its internal phase pattern to reconstruct the control logic. The device did not travel freely through time. It selected a destination state by locking onto a causal reference and resolving a path through the Veyr network's temporal coordinates. Without a stable anchor, the destination could drift.

AION could supply a reference state. That was the purpose of the Return Interlock: a coupling between the chamber's causal record and the Veyr device's destination mechanism. It was intended to bring any traveller back to the Stillhouse's local reference if the temporal transit failed to resolve.

But the recovered manual contained a warning that Adrian had not fully understood.

**INCOMPATIBLE TRAVELLER: RETURN TO NEAREST STABLE CAUSAL ANCHOR.**

He read it again.

“Nearest stable anchor,” he said.

Mira looked up. “What does that mean?”

“It may not mean AION. It may mean the nearest stable reference recognised by the Veyr system.”

“Which could be the destination period.”

“Or a Veyr network node. Or the last point the device can reconcile with the traveller's causal signature.”

Mira stared at the device. “So if it rejects us, it could return us somewhere else.”

“Yes.”

“Could it send me back to the invasion?”

“Possibly.”

“Could it strand me in the wrong history?”

“Possibly.”

She sat back. For the first time in days, the risk was no longer a number on the boundary display. It was a place she might be sent without him.

Adrian opened the interlock's control sequence. He could try to rewrite the return condition, but he did not know whether the Veyr device would accept the change. A faulty modification could prevent the jump entirely—or send the traveller into a causal state from which there was no return.

“We can test the interlock with an inert marker,” he said.

“And if it works?”

“We test it again.”

“And if it fails?”

“We don't use the device until we understand why.”

Mira looked at the capsule containing LANTERN. “How many tests do we have time for?”

He did not answer.

They ran the inert test. A small ceramic marker, tagged with AION's local reference signature, passed into the device's transit field and disappeared for 0.2 seconds by the chamber's clocks. It returned to the platform with a surface temperature change and a phase record that matched the expected displacement. The return condition had held.

The second test used a marker with a deliberately altered reference signature. The device rejected it, and the marker reappeared at the nearest stable point within the chamber's own field boundary. The test confirmed that the Return Interlock was not a simple recall mechanism. It sought a stable causal anchor compatible with the traveller's reference state.

“That gives us a safety margin,” Adrian said.

“It gives us a warning,” Mira replied. “The marker is not a person. We still don't know what the device will do with a living traveller.”

“Agreed.”

She placed the LANTERN capsule inside the transport container. “Then we need to decide who goes.”

He felt the answer before she said anything. The temporal device could only carry one traveller with the available power reserve. AION could preserve the remaining reference record, but the return path was uncertain. The virus had to be delivered into the earlier timeline, and someone had to choose the exact moment and place.

“I'll go,” Mira said.

“No.”

“Adrian—”

“I understand the biology. I can carry the sample and follow the deployment plan.”

“You understand the equations. You don't understand the pathogen's failure modes as well as I do.”

“You designed it. You can teach me.”

“Not enough. If the sample behaves differently in the earlier timeline, I need to recognise it. If the delivery fails, I need to decide whether to retry or abort.”

He searched for another argument. Every one he found was a way to protect her by pretending the mission could be done without her.

Mira softened her voice. “I'm not saying this because I want to leave you. I'm saying it because I'm the person who can give the mission its best chance.”

He looked at the Return Interlock warning.

“Then we make sure the device knows where to bring you back,” he said.

“Can you?”

“I don't know.”

She reached for his hand. “That's honest.”

He held it, feeling the warmth of her fingers and the terrible fact that their future now depended on a machine built from an enemy's damaged technology.

The virus was ready as far as they could make it ready.

The destination was not.
