# Chapter 22 — The Gift

The Veyr brought Mira to their scientific centre because they believed she was a discovery.

The facility occupied a hollow within a vast orbital structure, with laboratories arranged around a central chamber where biological and technological systems exchanged information through layers of translucent membranes. Veyr researchers moved between stations without speaking in any human sense. Their communication travelled through clicks, pulses, and contact with the living surfaces built into the architecture.

Mira was placed in a sealed observation room with a view of the central chamber. Her transit device remained locked in a separate containment vault. The LANTERN reservoir had been transferred twice since her capture, first to a cargo vessel, then to the orbital facility. Each transfer had moved it deeper into the Veyr's biological logistics network.

The examiners treated her with a mixture of caution and fascination. They took non-invasive scans, compared her cellular structure against their historical models, and ran simulations of the temporal field that had brought her into their past. Their records could not explain her presence. The more they studied her, the more the contradiction grew.

Mira used the time to observe.

The scientific centre's timing pulses were stronger than those of the colony node. The central chamber distributed a reference signal through living membranes that connected to the facility's power systems, research platforms, and transit controls. The same phase pattern appeared in the recovered navigation data and in the biological response she had measured before the temporal jump.

The anchor network was not a separate machine hidden somewhere beyond the stars. It was woven into the civilization's ordinary infrastructure. The Veyr had made their world dependent on the same biological coordination system that LANTERN was designed to disrupt.

Mira did not know whether the pathogen had activated. She had no access to the reservoir, no sample from the carriers, and no reliable way to distinguish an early infection from normal variation in the timing pulses. She refused to turn hope into evidence.

At the end of the third local cycle, the lead examiner entered with a display of the transit device's reconstructed field.

“Your machine uses a reference outside our historical model,” the translator said. “It may be capable of producing a stable contradiction.”

“Your own systems do that,” Mira replied.

The examiner's head tilted.

“Your temporal network maintains a history that excludes my species. Yet I am here. The contradiction exists whether you understand it or not.”

The examiner displayed a sequence of simulations. In each, Mira's presence created a branch that the system attempted to reconcile. The model returned to the same result: either her signature had to be classified as invalid, or the network's current history had to accept a state it had been designed to exclude.

“Why did you come?” the translator asked.

Mira looked at the central chamber. A researcher touched a membrane, and a pulse moved through the facility. The network was stable. The Veyr civilization had built its confidence on the assumption that the future could be controlled if enough of its dependencies were managed.

“To give you a choice,” she said.

The examiner did not understand the answer. It asked her to clarify.

Mira declined.

That night, the first confirmed biological anomaly appeared in the facility's routine health system. A Veyr technician's repair markers had fallen outside their normal synchronization range. The system classified the result as a minor metabolic irregularity and scheduled a follow-up scan. The technician remained active and continued working.

A second report appeared several hours later, from a different ship in another system. The symptom was similar, but the records treated it as an unrelated event. A third report followed from a biological cargo station. Each case was small, and none triggered a network-wide alert.

Mira saw the reports only because the examiners had left a monitoring display running in her observation room. She memorized the identifiers and the timing pattern. The changes were consistent with LANTERN's intended effect, but they were not proof that the pathogen had caused them.

She felt a rush of hope and forced herself to wait.

The Veyr's screening systems were designed to detect immediate threats: toxins, acute infections, foreign organisms, and biological instability. LANTERN's intended effect was delayed and indirect. It did not announce itself as a new disease. It interfered with a coordination pathway that the Veyr treated as part of ordinary physiology. Until the disruption became severe enough to affect multiple systems, each case could be explained away.

The FTL network carried personnel and biological cargo from one system to another. Each jump moved carriers, supplies, and the pathogen itself into new environments. The network was not transmitting infection across space. It was transporting infected living material faster than the Veyr's separate medical systems could recognise a shared outbreak.

Mira sat on the floor and leaned against the wall. She had no proof that the mission would succeed, but the first pieces of the chain were moving. The sample had reached the scientific centre. The biological anomalies were appearing in separate systems. The timing network continued to operate, yet its signals showed small, repeated losses of coherence.

She thought of Adrian's last words before she left: *Then we make sure the device knows where to bring you back.*

The return path remained uncertain. She did not know whether AION still existed in the rewritten history or whether Adrian was watching a stable clock. She did not know if the network would collapse or if she would die inside a Veyr laboratory before the mission reached its end.

But she had done what they planned. She had placed the pathogen inside the system that had erased humanity, and the Veyr had carried it onward because they believed they were transporting a scientific curiosity.

They had brought the gift into their own house.

The central chamber pulsed again. One membrane missed a beat, then recovered. The facility's systems corrected the error automatically.

Mira watched the pulse repeat.

For now, the network could repair itself.

The question was how many small failures it could survive before the whole civilization began to lose time.
