# Audit 3 — Grammar, Proofreading, and Language

**Scope:** prose drafts Chapters 1–24.  
**Editing level:** copyediting and consistency, without flattening the narrative voice.

## Corrections already applied on this branch

- **Chapter 1:** removed repeated “For the first time since the invasion” at the chapter's final beat; the two-line ending now escalates instead of repeating its opening.
- **Chapter 3:** corrected the invasion duration to nearly twenty years to match the established story timeline.
- **Chapters 17 and 19:** standardized the temporal destination wording to “the first recorded hostile arrival at Earth.”
- **Chapter 19:** changed “the civilization that had not yet arrived” to “the civilization that had not yet revealed itself to Earth,” consistent with a Veyr survey vessel already in the local system.
- **Chapters 23–24:** clarified that the Return Interlock preserves a causal reference, not a guaranteed physical destination.

## House style recommendation

Use **British English** consistently, which suits the Sri Lankan setting:
- centre, defence, travelled, travelling, recognised, colour, labelled, modelling;
- retain official names, quoted source text, or deliberate character voice where a different spelling is meaningful;
- use 24-hour times consistently, as the drafts already mostly do;
- italicize internal thoughts only when they are clearly presented as direct thought, not for every emphasis;
- use em dashes consistently and avoid mixing hyphens and dashes for the same parenthetical function;
- preserve scientific terms in dialogue, but define or paraphrase them when a reader needs them to follow the scene.

## Language patterns to control

### 1. Repeated rhetorical constructions
Across the manuscript, recurring constructions include:
- “Not X. Not Y. …”
- “It was not … It was …”
- “For the first time …”
- “He did not know …”
- “The question was …”
- “It could be …”
- “That was not the same as …”

These are effective motifs in moderation, especially for a story about contradictory histories. In clusters, they begin to sound like a repeated template. Keep the strongest instance in a scene and vary the rest with physical action, specific observation, or a shorter sentence.

### 2. Repeated uncertainty dialogue
Adrian and Mira appropriately avoid false certainty, but many scenes cycle through “maybe,” “possibly,” “we don't know,” and “not yet.” Preserve their scientific caution while making each exchange advance the hypothesis. If a character has already stated the uncertainty, the next line should add evidence, a decision, or a consequence rather than restating it.

### 3. Abstract nouns can accumulate
Terms such as *reference, state, system, signal, phase, network, history,* and *causal* are necessary to the premise. Pair them with physical evidence: a clock jumps, a membrane misses a pulse, a light fails, a page changes, or a person makes a choice. The draft is strongest when the abstract explanation follows an observable event.

### 4. Dialogue attribution and voice
Adrian's precision and Mira's pointed humour are distinct and readable. Avoid giving both characters the same polished aphoristic voice in every exchange. Let Adrian occasionally be emotionally blunt and let Mira occasionally be uncertain or wrong; it will keep them human rather than turning each into a spokesperson for the theme.

### 5. Proofreading checklist
- Check subject–verb agreement in sentences with long technical subjects.
- Check pronoun antecedents when “it” could refer to the device, network, history, or signal.
- Keep past-perfect tense for events preceding the scene; move back to simple past once the chronology is established.
- Standardize capitalization for AION, the Stillhouse, LANTERN, Veyr, and FTL.
- Standardize “Veyr temporal device,” “temporal anchor,” “anchor node,” and “network” so synonyms do not imply separate technologies accidentally.
- Ensure each scene's timestamps are internally ordered unless the discrepancy is a deliberate temporal clue.
- Check commas around dialogue tags and sentence fragments. Fragments are a deliberate stylistic device in the novel; correct only those that look accidental.

## Sample line-editing principle

Prefer a concrete image plus one interpretation over three consecutive interpretations. For example, when a clock misses a pulse, show the missed pulse and the character's response first; explain the causal significance once the reader has something physical to hold on to.

This is a manuscript-level proofreading audit. A final publication copyedit should still be done after structural revisions, because moving or rewriting scenes will create new grammar and continuity issues.
